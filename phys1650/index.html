          Chapter 1

          Units in astronomy

          In one breath: Astronomers measure lengths in metres (and bigger yardsticks built from them), times in seconds and years, and angles in degrees, arcminutes and arcseconds. Sizes in the universe span 40 powers of ten, so the habit you need is to think in ratios and powers of ten, not in raw numbers.

          Lecture notes: Chapter 1

            Length scales: powers of ten

            From the notes
            • Sizes range from the nucleus (10⁻¹⁴ m) to the universe (above 10²⁶ m).
            • Key rungs: atom 10⁻¹⁰ m · bacterium 10⁻⁶ m · hair 10⁻⁴ m · human 10⁰ m · building 10² m · Earth 10⁷ m · Sun 10⁹ m · solar system 10¹³ m · light-year 10¹⁶ m · globular cluster or nebula 10¹⁸ m · galaxy 10²¹ m · cluster of galaxies 10²⁴ m · quasars 10²⁶ m.
            • The Sun compared with the Earth is about like a human compared with a bee.

            Each step of 10 on this ladder is “ten times bigger”. To compare any two things, subtract their exponents. Sun (10⁹) over Earth (10⁷) = 10² = “about a hundred”; the true value is about 109 times.

            Think like the exam

            You are rarely asked to recite the ladder. You are asked to use it: “How many Earths fit across the Sun?” (about 110), “How many Suns fit across the solar system?” (10¹³ ÷ 10⁹ = 10⁴), “How many light-years is 10²¹ m?” (10²¹ ÷ 10¹⁶ = 10⁵). Always subtract exponents, then sanity-check against the picture of the objects.

            Time scales

            From the notes
            • Age of the universe 1.4 × 10¹⁰ yr; galaxies form 10¹⁰ yr ago; solar system 4.6 × 10⁹ yr; first life 3 × 10⁹ yr; Cambrian animals 6 × 10⁸ yr; dinosaurs 0.65 to 2.5 × 10⁸ yr ago; early hominids 3 × 10⁶ yr; Homo sapiens 3 × 10⁵ yr; written history 5 × 10³ yr; a human life 10² yr.

            Angular size

            From the notes
            • Angular size is the angle an object spans as seen by the observer. Halve the distance → double the angular size.
            • Full circle = 360°; 1° = 60′ (arcminutes); 1′ = 60″ (arcseconds). The Moon is about 0.5° across.
            • At arm’s length: fingertip ≈ 1°, fist ≈ 10°, outstretched hand (thumb tip to little finger tip) ≈ 20°. The Moon is smaller than your finger.
            angle (radians) = diameter ÷ distance   ·   angle (degrees) = 57.3 × diameter ÷ distance
            Think like the exam

            Three uses recur: (1) Find the angle from a size and a distance (Sun from Earth: 2 × 6.96 × 10⁸ ÷ 1.5 × 10¹¹ = 9.3 × 10⁻³ rad = 0.53°). (2) Find the distance from a size and an angle. (3) Compare two objects with ratios: B’s distance = A’s distance × (size ratio ÷ angle ratio), as in Assignment 1 Q1.

            Units: use the same unit on top and bottom, and convert radians to degrees at the end. Arcminutes and arcseconds in angles are not the same as minutes and seconds of time (this matters again in Chapter 3).

            Practise

            Your fingertip at arm’s length covers the Moon with room to spare. What does that tell you about the Moon’s angular size?
            The fingertip is about 1° and the Moon is about 0.5°, so the Moon is about half the width of your fingertip.
            A building 100 m tall is seen at 2° angular height. How far is it?
            d = size ÷ angle = 100 ÷ (2 × π/180) = 100 ÷ 0.0349 ≈ 2900 m ≈ 2.9 km.
            Convert 0.53° to arcminutes and arcseconds.
            0.53 × 60 = 31.8′; 31.8 × 60 = 1908″.
            A galaxy 10²¹ m across: how many light-years is that? (1 ly ≈ 10¹⁶ m)
            10²¹ ÷ 10¹⁶ = 10⁵ light-years, about 100,000 ly.

            Chapter 2

            Motions of heavenly bodies

            In one breath: The Earth spins west to east, so everything in the sky seems to rise in the east and set in the west. The Earth also orbits the Sun once a year, so the Sun slides eastward along the ecliptic against the stars, the planets wander, and the tilt of the Earth’s axis (23.5°) gives us seasons and different sunrise directions.

            Lecture notes: Chapter 2 · Test 1 and Assignment 1

              The daily motion of the stars

              From the notes
              • Imagine the Earth at the centre of a celestial sphere carrying the Sun, stars and planets. Because the Earth rotates west to east, everything seems to move east to west.
              • Away from the equator and poles, the Earth’s axis is tilted from the horizon, so stars near the north celestial pole never set: circumpolar stars. Polaris is within a hair of the pole, so it looks still. In Hong Kong it is about 22.4° above due north.
              • People in the northern hemisphere never see stars near the south celestial pole, and southerners never see Polaris.
              Think like the exam

              One rule runs the whole topic: altitude of the celestial pole = your latitude. From it: (1) the circumpolar limit is declination 90° − latitude (67.5° in Hong Kong, Assignment 1 Q4). (2) At the equator the pole is on the horizon, so every star rises vertically. (3) At the pole the axis is vertical, so stars circle parallel to the horizon. Test 1 Q1 asks for exactly these, and for the angle from vertical at which a due-east star rises (it equals the latitude).

              The Sun’s yearly motion and what a “day” is

              From the notes
              • Stars are fixed on the sphere (apart from tiny proper motions, Chapter 12). The Sun moves west to east, one full circle a year, along the ecliptic. The twelve constellations it crosses are the zodiac (it also crosses Ophiuchus, which is left out for historical reasons). The Sun does not spend equal time in each.
              • Solar day: the Sun returns to the same place relative to the ground (midday to midday). Sidereal day: other stars come back to the same positions. Because of the orbit, a solar day is longer than a sidereal day: a year has about 365 solar days but 366 sidereal days.

              Each day the Earth also moves about 1° along its orbit, so after one full turn the Sun is still 1° away from where it was; the Earth needs about 4 more minutes of turning to face the Sun again. That extra turn over a year adds up to exactly one extra rotation: 366 turns in 365 days.

              Think like the exam

              Formula for a spinning planet: 1/solar = 1/sidereal − 1/year if it spins the same way as it orbits, and + if opposite. Earth: 1/23.93 h − 1/8766 h gives 24 h. If the Earth were reversed (Assignment 1 Q7), the sign flips: the solar day is shorter than the sidereal day. Venus (retrograde) has a solar day (117 d) much shorter than its sidereal day (243 d); Mercury (176 d) is longer than its 59-day spin.

              The calendar

              From the notes
              • A year is 365.2422 solar days. Leap years add 29 February. Gregorian rule: divisible by 400 → leap; else divisible by 100 → not leap; else divisible by 4 → leap; else not. (2000 and 2400 yes; 1800, 1900, 2100 no.) Error stays under one day for 3000 years.
              • The synodic month (full moon to full moon) is 29.5 days. The Chinese calendar is based on both the Moon and the Sun.
              Think like the exam

              Counting questions need a clean plan. Multiples of 4 in the range, then subtract the centuries that fail the 400 rule (1600–2500: 226 − 7 = 219). Check whether the end years themselves count. Also notice the link to phases: a lunar month starts at new moon, so day 1 is always near new moon and day 15 is near full moon (Assignment 2 Q4).

              Seasons, equinoxes and solstices

              From the notes
              • The Earth’s rotation axis is tilted 23.5° from the axis of its orbit. The celestial equator is the projection of the Earth’s equator; the ecliptic crosses it at 23.5°.
              • The two crossings are the vernal and autumnal equinoxes; the farthest points from the equator are the summer and winter solstices. The Sun reaches them about 20 March, 21 June, 23 September and 22 December.
              • At the summer solstice the Sun shines over the northern hemisphere (summer there, winter in the south). It rises at its northernmost point, giving long days and short shadows; at the winter solstice, the reverse.
              Trap

              Seasons are caused by the tilt, not by distance. The Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January. If distance were the reason, the whole Earth would have the same season at once, but the two hemispheres are always opposite.

              Motions of the planets

              From the notes
              • To the ancients, planets were wandering “stars”. There are eight major planets plus dwarf planets and minor planets.
              • Inferior planets (Mercury, Venus) orbit inside the Earth’s orbit, so they never stray far from the Sun and are seen only before sunrise (“morning star”) or after sunset (“evening star”).
              • Superior planets (Mars to Neptune) move west to east most of the time (prograde). Because the Earth moves faster, it sometimes catches up and overtakes them and they seem to move east to west (retrograde), for weeks to months. Inferior planets also have both kinds of motion.
              Think like the exam

              Retrograde is a relative-motion effect, not the planet really reversing. The only quick description to learn: “as the Earth overtakes a slower outer planet, our line of sight to it swings backward against the stars”. A planet is retrograde near opposition, when it is opposite the Sun and highest at midnight. It lasts weeks to months, so you cannot see it in one night.

              Practise

              Why does Polaris stay in the same place all night?
              It is almost exactly at the north celestial pole, on the Earth’s rotation axis, so the rotation hardly moves it.
              What is the altitude of the north celestial pole at latitude 40° N?
              40° above the due-north horizon.
              Why is the solar day longer than the sidereal day?
              The Earth moves along its orbit while it spins, so after one full spin it must turn a little further (about 1°, 4 minutes) to bring the Sun back to the same position.
              Is 2100 a leap year? Is 2400?
              2100: divisible by 100 but not 400 → no. 2400: divisible by 400 → yes.
              In which direction does the Sun rise at the autumnal equinox, and then toward which direction does it move?
              Due east, then moves toward the south of east until the winter solstice.

              Quick check

              Chapter 2 must-know list

              • Spin gives daily east-to-west motion; pole altitude = latitude; circumpolar if declination ≥ 90° − latitude.
              • Ecliptic, zodiac, solar day vs sidereal day (366 sidereal days in 365 solar days).
              • Gregorian rule; synodic month 29.5 d.
              • Tilt 23.5°; equinoxes and solstices; sunrise directions; Sun overhead twice a year in the tropics.
              • Inferior vs superior planets; prograde vs retrograde and why.

              Chapter 3

              Celestial sphere and star atlas

              In one breath: Put yourself at the centre of an imaginary sphere with every star painted on it. Give the sphere a coordinate grid like latitude and longitude: declination (north–south, ±90°) and right ascension (0 to 24 hours, starting at the vernal equinox). Then any star has an address, and a star atlas is just a map of the inside of that sphere.

              Lecture notes: Chapter 3

                The celestial sphere and its coordinates

                From the notes
                • We cannot judge the distance to stars, so they all seem to lie on one surface: the celestial sphere, centred on us. It does not turn with the Earth: stars are fixed on it.
                • The projections of the Earth’s axis are the north and south celestial poles; the projection of the Earth’s equator is the celestial equator.
                • Declination (like latitude): +90° at the north pole to −90° at the south pole. Right ascension (like longitude): 0 to 24 hours, west to east, starting at the vernal equinox (where the ecliptic crosses the celestial equator). An hour has 60 minutes and a minute 60 seconds, but these are not arcminutes and arcseconds.

                24 hours of right ascension cover 360°, so 1 hour = 15°; 1 minute of RA = 15 arcminutes; 1 second of RA = 15 arcseconds. That is the whole difference between RA “minutes” and angle “minutes”.

                Think like the exam

                Combine this chapter with Chapter 2: for a northern observer at latitude φ, a star with declination δ is circumpolar if δ ≥ 90° − φ, never rises if δ ≤ −(90° − φ), and otherwise rises and sets. When it crosses the meridian its altitude is 90° − |φ − δ|. For example, a star at δ = +90° is the pole: altitude = φ. Sirius (δ ≈ −17°) from Hong Kong: 90° − (22.3° + 17°) = 50.7°.

                Remarks: celestial north, precession

                From the notes
                • Celestial north is not the magnetic north that a compass points to; the angle between them depends on where you are.
                • Like a spinning top, the Earth’s axis slowly traces a circle in space, period about 26,000 years: precession. So the vernal equinox and the pole star change with time, and coordinates must quote a date (e.g. 2000.0).

                Constellations

                From the notes
                • Constellations are imaginary patterns made by ancient people; each culture had its own. Before 1922 their boundaries were fuzzy. In 1922 the IAU divided the sky into 88 constellations with sharp boundaries; each star lies in exactly one.
                • The zodiac is the set of constellations the ecliptic passes through (Ophiuchus not included).
                • Around January the Sun is in Sagittarius; in the evening we see Taurus and Gemini, the “winter constellations”. Other seasons show other constellations.
                Think like the exam

                The constellations you can see at night are the ones opposite the Sun’s current position on the ecliptic. The Sun moves 360° in a year, about 1° a day, so the evening sky shifts by about 2 hours of RA per month. Stars rise about 4 minutes earlier each night (the sidereal-day effect from Chapter 2).

                Star atlas

                From the notes
                • With an ordinary map you look down; with a star map you lie down and look up. So east and west are reversed compared with a road map.
                • A bigger dot on a star map shows a brighter star, not a larger one (brightness is discussed in Chapters 5 and 12).
                Trap

                If a question asks “why are east and west reversed on a star map?”, the answer is the viewpoint: on a star map the observer faces up at the sky, but on a street map the observer faces down at the ground. Do not say that the map is wrong or that the stars move.

                Practise

                A star has right ascension 6 h 30 min. How many degrees along the equator from the vernal equinox is that?
                6.5 h × 15° per hour = 97.5°.
                Can an observer in Hong Kong (22.3° N) see a star at declination −75°?
                No: it never rises, because −75° is south of −(90° − 22.3°) = −67.7°.
                Is Polaris’s declination about +90° or 0°? What is its altitude in Hong Kong?
                About +89°, close to +90°, so its altitude is about the latitude, 22°.
                Why must coordinates quote a year, e.g. “J2000.0”?
                Precession slowly moves the celestial poles and the vernal equinox (period 26,000 years), so the grid shifts.

                Quick check

                Chapter 3 must-know list

                • Celestial sphere (fixed stars), poles, equator, declination (±90°), RA (0–24 h from the vernal equinox; 1 h = 15°).
                • Celestial north ≠ magnetic north; precession 26,000 yr.
                • 88 constellations since 1922; zodiac; seasonal constellations.
                • Star atlases: look up, so east/west reversed; bigger dot = brighter.

                Chapter 4

                Brief history of astronomy

                In one breath: The Greeks built an Earth-centred universe of perfect circles; Ptolemy patched it with epicycles to explain retrograde motion. Copernicus put the Sun at the centre; Galileo’s telescope observations, especially Venus’s phases, supported it; Kepler found the three laws of planetary motion; Newton explained them all with gravity.

                Lecture notes: Chapter 4 · Test 1 and Assignment 1

                  Early astronomy and the Ptolemaic model

                  From the notes
                  • Ancient China: the heavens were important but mythical, so there were serious, detailed records but no significant predictive models.
                  • ~600 BC Thales: the universe is rational and humans can understand it. ~400 BC Plato: the heavens are perfect, and the circle is the perfect shape, so motion is uniform and circular around the Earth: the start of the geocentric model.
                  • The simple geocentric model cannot explain retrograde motion. About 140 AD Ptolemy: the planet moves on a small circle (epicycle), whose centre moves on a larger circle (deferent) around the Earth. The epicycle centres of Mercury and Venus must lie on the line joining Earth and Sun. Stars are fixed on an outer sphere.
                  • Predictions within a few degrees: accepted for about 1500 years.
                  Think like the exam

                  Know what a model must do: explain observations and predict. Ptolemy’s model does explain retrograde motion (with epicycles) and predicts positions to a few degrees. What it cannot do is explain the full phases of Venus. That is the point behind Assignment 1 Q8.

                  Copernicus and Galileo

                  From the notes
                  • Copernicus (1473–1543): heliocentric model. The Sun is the centre; Earth is just another planet. It is simple, elegant, and predicts retrograde motion, but its predictions were only about as good as Ptolemy’s, so it was not commonly accepted at once.
                  • Galileo (1564–1642) did not invent the telescope and was not burnt; he was condemned for “doing science” (observing and experimenting versus faith) and confined to his villa for his last ten years. He was the first to use a telescope on the sky.
                  • Four discoveries: (1) mountains on the Moon; (2) sunspots; together, the heavens are not perfect. (3) Four satellites of Jupiter: there are other “centres”. (4) Venus goes through a full set of phases, including full. If Venus were on an epicycle between Earth and Sun, we could only see a crescent. So Venus orbits the Sun.
                  Think like the exam

                  Match each discovery with the idea it kills: mountains + sunspots → the heavens are perfect; Jupiter’s moons → everything circles the Earth; Venus’s phases → Venus’s centre of motion is the Sun (not an epicycle on the Earth–Sun line). Venus’s phases are the discovery that directly separates the two models.

                  Kepler’s three laws

                  From the notes
                  • Tycho Brahe measured planet positions almost daily; his assistant Kepler used the data.
                  • First law: orbits are ellipses with the Sun at one focus. (Draw one with a string and two pins.)
                  • Second law: a line from planet to Sun sweeps equal areas in equal times, so a planet moves faster when nearer the Sun.
                  • Third law: (period)² = constant × (average distance)³. The constant is the same for everything orbiting the Sun (planets, comets, satellites). For the Moon or satellites around the Earth, use a different constant.
                  Think like the exam

                  With P in years and a in AU the constant is 1: P² = a³. Use ratios when the star is different (Assignment 1 Q10: (aB/aA)³ = (PB/PA)²). Halley: a = 762/3 = 17.9 AU; farthest distance = 2a − perihelion. Moon orbits the Earth: do not reuse the Sun’s constant.

                  Newton

                  From the notes
                  • Newton deduced Kepler’s three laws (and more) from fewer assumptions. Any two massive bodies attract: F = G M₁M₂ / r². G is very small, which is why we do not feel the attraction between two people.
                  • The Moon does not fly away because it constantly “falls” toward the Earth, just as a pen does. Planets “fall” toward the Sun.
                  • Orbits can be any of the four conic sections: circle, ellipse, parabola, hyperbola, and also straight lines.
                  • Epilogue: relativity later modified Newton’s theory. Science is a cycle: propose, test, refine.
                  Think like the exam

                  Gravity questions are scaling questions. Doubling one mass doubles F; doubling distance gives ¼; the pull on the Earth by the Moon is equal and opposite to the Moon’s pull by the Earth (Assignment 1 Q9: balance point where the pulls match, r/(R−r) = √(Me/Mm) = 9).

                  Practise

                  Which of Galileo’s discoveries directly shows that Venus orbits the Sun?
                  Its full set of phases, including a (nearly) full Venus.
                  Name two things Galileo discovered that showed the heavens are not perfect.
                  Mountains and craters on the Moon, and sunspots.
                  A planet’s orbit has period 8 years. What is its average distance from the Sun?
                  a = 82/3 = 4 AU.
                  At perihelion, does a planet move faster or slower than at aphelion? Which law?
                  Faster, by Kepler’s second law (equal areas in equal times).
                  Heliocentric and Ptolemaic models both predict planet positions within a few degrees. Why did people still prefer one over the other for 1500 years?
                  The Ptolemaic model matched everyday experience (the Earth does not seem to move), the Greek idea of perfect circles, and later religious belief. Only new evidence (Galileo, Kepler, Newton) shifted opinion.

                  Quick check

                  Chapter 4 must-know list

                  • Ptolemy: epicycle + deferent, ~140 AD, 1500 years; Mercury/Venus centres on the Earth–Sun line.
                  • Copernicus: heliocentric, simple, predicts retrograde motion, not better at prediction.
                  • Galileo: Moon mountains, sunspots, Jupiter’s four moons, Venus’s full phases.
                  • Kepler’s laws: ellipse; equal areas; P² ∝ a³. Newton: F = GM₁M₂/r²; four conic sections.

                  Chapter 5

                  Telescopes and electromagnetic waves

                  In one breath: A telescope is mainly a light bucket: a bigger lens or mirror collects more light and gives sharper images, but it does not magnify more. Light is an electromagnetic wave; hot, dense objects glow with a black-body spectrum whose peak colour tells their temperature; spectral lines identify gases; the Doppler shift gives motion; and magnitude is the log scale of brightness.

                  Lecture notes: Chapter 5 · Test 1 and Assignment 2

                    Why we need a telescope

                    From the notes
                    • Most objects are dim. A telescope collects more light and brings it to a focus. A larger collecting area (bigger main mirror or lens) is more powerful. Keck has two 10 m mirrors; larger ones are planned.
                    • Large telescopes also improve resolution: we see more clearly, like the difference between in focus and out of focus.
                    • Three kinds: refractor (a lens bends light to a focus), reflector (a mirror), catadioptric (a correcting lens plate plus a mirror). Extra mirrors bring the focus to a convenient place.
                    • Misconception: a larger telescope does not magnify more. Magnification = ratio of apparent (angular) sizes; for example 1′ becoming 30′ is a magnification of 30. It is changed by the eyepiece. It is seldom over 500, usually 100–200. Large telescopes show brighter and sharper images.
                    • “How far can you see?” is not well defined: the right question is “how dim an object can you see?”
                    • The mount must be stable and must track the stars, compensating the Earth’s rotation. There are telescopes for radio, infrared, X-rays and gamma rays, on the ground or in orbit.
                    Think like the exam

                    Test 1 Q3 is this section: (a) the primary element of a refractor is a lens; (b) magnification = angular size of the image ÷ angular size of the object; (c) very large magnification gives a large but fuzzy image because the resolution and the atmosphere limit the detail. A good pattern for “why build big telescopes?” is: collect more light (see fainter things, area ∝ D²) and better resolution; not “bigger magnification”.

                    Light as a wave

                    From the notes
                    • (wavelength) × (frequency) = c for electromagnetic waves in vacuum.
                    • Visible light spans 400 nm to 700 nm. Infrared and radio have longer wavelengths and lower frequencies; ultraviolet and gamma rays have shorter wavelengths and higher frequencies.
                    • The atmosphere is opaque to gamma rays and transparent to visible light, so gamma-ray telescopes must be in space.
                    Think like the exam

                    Worked: green light 500 nm has frequency c/λ = 3 × 10⁸ / 5 × 10⁻⁷ = 6 × 10¹⁴ Hz. An FM radio wave at 100 MHz has λ = 3 × 10⁸ / 10⁸ = 3 m. Order: radio → infrared → visible (red … violet) → ultraviolet → X-ray → gamma. Shorter wavelength = higher frequency = more energetic.

                    Black-body radiation: colour and temperature

                    From the notes
                    • A hot, dense object (a star, a piece of metal) radiates at many wavelengths; its spectrum is a black-body spectrum.
                    • The spectrum depends only on the surface temperature. Low temperature: the peak is in the red; hotter: the peak moves to yellow, blue and then ultraviolet. A flat spectrum looks white.
                    • Hotter objects also radiate more energy per unit area. So colour is mainly set by surface temperature, but luminosity depends on both surface temperature and surface area.
                    Think like the exam

                    Assignment 2 Q2 asks how to find a star’s surface temperature: locate the peak wavelength and use Wien’s law T = 2.9 × 10⁶ nm·K ÷ λmax (the Sun’s peak of about 500 nm gives about 5800 K). Two more reasoning habits:

                    • Same temperature, different size: same colour, but the larger star is more luminous. L ∝ (surface area) × T⁴ ∝ R²T⁴. A red giant is red (cool) yet very luminous because it is huge.

                    • Same size, different temperature: the hotter star is bluer and much more luminous: doubling T gives 16 times more energy per unit area.

                    Spectral lines

                    From the notes
                    • Real spectra can have absorption lines or emission lines.
                    • Low-pressure, low-density gas (such as a star’s outer atmosphere) absorbs light at particular wavelengths and re-radiates it in random directions. White light passing through it gets dark absorption lines; viewed from the side, the same gas shows emission lines.
                    • Each gas has its own set of lines, so the line pattern tells us which gas it is.
                    Think like the exam

                    Remember the three conditions: hot dense body → continuous (black-body) spectrum; cool thin gas in front of it → absorption lines; hot thin gas seen alone → emission lines (like the red glow of an ionised nebula, Chapter 13). Lines are fingerprints.

                    The Doppler effect

                    From the notes
                    • A source approaching gives a higher frequency (shorter wavelength): blue-shifted. A source leaving gives lower frequency (longer wavelength): red-shifted.
                    • For speeds much smaller than c: (observed − original wavelength) ÷ original wavelength = v / c, where v is the speed with which the source is leaving.
                    • Comparing a star’s absorption lines with laboratory lines tells us whether it is moving toward or away and how fast.
                    Think like the exam

                    Worked: a line expected at 656.3 nm is observed at 656.52 nm. Δλ/λ = 0.22 / 656.3 = 3.4 × 10⁻⁴, so v = 3.4 × 10⁻⁴ × 3 × 10⁵ km/s ≈ 100 km/s, receding (red-shifted). The Doppler effect measures only motion along the line of sight, not sideways (sideways motion is “proper motion”, Chapter 12).

                    Stellar brightness: magnitude

                    From the notes
                    • Visual (apparent) magnitude measures how bright a star looks. It is a log scale of intensity (energy received).
                    • If star A is 100 times as bright as star B, then mB − mA = 5. A dimmer star has a larger magnitude number.
                    • One magnitude is a factor 1001/5 = 2.51. A magnitude-5 star is 100 times dimmer than a magnitude-0 star. The formula: m = −(5/2) log₁₀(I / I₀), with I₀ the intensity of the zero-magnitude reference.

                    Chapter 6

                    The solar system and the Earth

                    In one breath: The solar system formed from a rotating cloud of gas and dust that collapsed and flattened into a disk, with the Sun at the centre and planets forming in the disk. The Earth is a layered, magnetic, plate-tectonic planet with an atmosphere that traps heat and scatters blue light, and with tides caused by the Moon (and the Sun).

                    Lecture notes: Chapter 6 · Assignment 2

                      How the solar system formed

                      From the notes
                      • We cannot go back or repeat it, so the story is an educated guess: it must satisfy all known laws and be natural, not the result of special initial arrangements.
                      • About 5 billion years ago a large cloud of gas and dust contracted under its own gravity. It also rotated, and a rotating object with no outside force keeps rotating. Rotation and collisions between particles flattened it into an accretion disk.
                      • Material at the centre formed the Sun; material elsewhere contracted into planets. The cloud’s rotation became the planets’ revolutions and the Sun’s spin. The whole process took about one billion years.
                      Think like the exam

                      Use the story to explain facts: planets orbit in the same direction and nearly the same plane because they formed from one flattened, rotating disk. The Sun’s spin is in that same direction. Flattening happens because rotation prevents collapse sideways but not along the axis.

                      Overview of the solar system

                      From the notes
                      • The Sun is about 1000 times more massive than everything else in the solar system combined.
                      • Eight planets from the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune. Pluto is a dwarf planet.
                      • 1 AU (average Earth–Sun distance) ≈ 1.5 × 10¹¹ m. The ratio of the Earth’s diameter to 1 AU is about 1 : 10,000: planets are tiny compared with their separations. There are also asteroids, comets and much more.
                      Think like the exam

                      Scale sense helps with estimates: light takes about 8 minutes to cross 1 AU (1.5 × 10¹¹ ÷ 3 × 10⁸ ≈ 500 s), and about 4 hours to Neptune (30 AU).

                      The Earth

                      From the notes
                      • Mass about 6 × 10²⁴ kg; radius about 6400 km (light goes round 7.5 times in a second); highest mountain about 9000 m (tiny compared with the radius); density about 5 times water.
                      • One natural satellite: the Moon.
                      • Layers: core (iron and nickel; inner core solid, outer core liquid; heat from natural radioactivity), mantle (partially melted), crust (solid).
                      • The crust is broken into plates, thousands of km wide but only about 50 km thick, floating on a hot, soft layer: plate tectonics.
                      • The rotation of the core makes the magnetic field; its axis is tilted from the spin axis and offset from the centre. The field traps charged particles from the Sun; when they hit atoms in the air they glow: aurora. Aurora on other planets mean they too have a magnetic field and an atmosphere.
                      • Atmosphere: 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, plus argon and carbon dioxide, etc.
                      • Greenhouse effect: gases such as CO₂ and SO₂ trap infrared radiation and raise the temperature; the strength depends on the amount of greenhouse gas. (Chapter 8: Venus.)
                      • Turbulence makes stars twinkle. The air scatters blue light more than red, so the setting Sun looks red: the red light comes straight to us, and most blue is scattered away.
                      Think like the exam

                      The Earth’s density (5.5) is the reference for Chapters 8 and 9: rocky planets are near 4–5.5, gas giants near 1 or less. The core’s rotation makes the magnetic field, so a planet with a fast-rotating liquid, conducting interior (Jupiter, Chapter 9) has a strong field. A sunset is red for the same reason the daytime sky is blue: air scatters blue light most.

                      Tides

                      From the notes
                      • The Moon pulls hardest on water on the near side of the Earth, less on the Earth itself, least on the far-side water. Relative to the Earth’s centre this gives two bulges of water: so two high tides a day.
                      • The Sun also pulls on the water. At new moon and full moon the Sun’s and Moon’s effects reinforce each other, so the high tides are higher than usual.
                      Think like the exam

                      The key fact is that tides come from the difference in the pull across the Earth, not just the pull itself, which is why there are two bulges, not one. This is what makes Assignment 2 Q3 work: the number of bulges is set by the geometry, and two moons on opposite sides give the same two bulges, only higher. Tides are highest at new and full moon (spring tides) and weakest at the quarters (neap).

                      Practise

                      Why do the planets orbit in almost the same plane and the same direction?
                      They formed from the same flattened, rotating disk of gas and dust.
                      Why is the setting Sun red?
                      Blue light is scattered out of the line of sight by the long path through the air; red light, scattered less, reaches us directly.
                      What causes the Earth’s magnetic field, and what is an aurora?
                      The rotation of the core. Aurora: charged particles from the Sun are trapped by the field and make air atoms and molecules glow when they collide.
                      Why are there two high tides a day and not one?
                      The Moon’s pull is strongest on the near side water and weakest on the far side water; relative to the Earth’s centre this gives a bulge on each side.
                      Name two times in the month when the high tides are highest.
                      New moon and full moon, when the Sun and Moon pull along the same line.

                      Quick check

                      Chapter 6 must-know list

                      • Nebular theory: rotating collapsing cloud → disk → Sun + planets; about one billion years.
                      • Sun ≈ 1000 × everything else; 1 AU = 1.5 × 10¹¹ m.
                      • Earth: density ≈ 5.5; core/mantle/crust; plates; magnetic field → aurora; 78% N₂, 21% O₂; greenhouse effect.
                      • Blue scattered more: red sunset. Two bulges, two high tides a day; springs at new/full moon.

                      Chapter 7

                      The Moon and eclipses

                      In one breath: The Moon is a dead, cratered, airless world that always shows us the same face. Its phases come from our changing view of its sunlit half. A lunar eclipse happens at full moon when the Moon enters the Earth’s shadow; a solar eclipse happens at new moon when the Moon’s shadow touches the Earth. They are rare only because the Moon’s orbit is tilted.

                      Lecture notes: Chapter 7 · Assignment 2

                        The Moon

                        From the notes
                        • Earth’s only natural satellite. Average distance = 30 Earth diameters; radius ≈ ¼ of the Earth’s; mass = 1/81 of the Earth’s. Essentially no atmosphere.
                        • Dark areas: maria (seas), solidified lava that flowed after the crust formed. Most “hills” are craters from impacts. The large number of craters shows very little lunar activity: the Moon is “dead”. Simple craters are bowl-shaped; complex craters have a central hill from rebound.
                        • The only other world humans have visited: six crewed Apollo landings, late 1960s to early 1970s.
                        • Time between new moons (synodic period) = 29.5 days. The Moon’s rotation period relative to the Earth is exactly the same: synchronous rotation, so we see only the near side. Its centre of mass is closer to the Earth than its geometric centre, which together with gravity locks the rotation.
                        • Formation: binary accretion (from the same cloud as the Earth) or the more popular giant impact (a Mars-sized object hit the young Earth and material from both formed the Moon).
                        Think like the exam

                        Use the numbers: the Moon’s surface gravity is much smaller than the Earth’s (mass 1/81, radius ¼: g ∝ M/R² = (1/81) ÷ (1/16) ≈ 0.2). With no atmosphere there is no weather to erase craters. The Moon’s rotation and its orbit have the same period, so the same side always faces us (it is not “not rotating”).

                        Phases

                        From the notes
                        • The Moon only reflects sunlight, so the amount of its surface that is lit as we see it, its phase, changes. The cycle: new moon → waxing crescent → first quarter → waxing gibbous → full moon → waning gibbous → third quarter → waning crescent → new moon.
                        Think like the exam

                        Phases are not caused by the Earth’s shadow. Half of the Moon is always lit; the phase is the fraction of that lit half that faces us. At new moon the Moon is between the Earth and the Sun (near the Sun in the sky), at full moon the Earth is between them. A crescent moon is therefore always near the Sun in the sky, which is why it is seen low, soon after sunset or before sunrise.

                        Lunar eclipses

                        From the notes
                        • A lunar eclipse happens when the Moon falls into the Earth’s shadow. The shadow has the umbra (the Sun totally blocked) and the penumbra (partly blocked).
                        • Moon in the umbra → total lunar eclipse; partly in the umbra → partial; only in the penumbra → usually called no eclipse (from the Moon you would see a partial solar eclipse).
                        • A totally eclipsed Moon is much dimmer than a partially eclipsed one.
                        • Lunar eclipses need the Moon “behind” the Earth. Why not every month? The lunar orbit plane is not the same as the Earth’s orbital plane, so at most full moons the Moon is north or south of the Earth’s shadow. The same reason means no solar eclipse every new moon.
                        Think like the exam

                        Assignment 2 Q4(a): “Lunar eclipses never occur during a crescent moon”: true, because a lunar eclipse needs a full moon. The extra numbers (tilt about 5°) are not in the notes; the idea is.

                        Solar eclipses

                        From the notes
                        • Same as lunar with the roles reversed: a solar eclipse occurs when the Moon is between the Sun and Earth (new moon) and its shadow falls on us. The most common is the partial solar eclipse. (Never look at the Sun without protection.)
                        • The path of a total eclipse is very narrow, so staying in one place, you probably will not see even one in your lifetime. People travel to see them.
                        • Before totality, the uneven edge of the Moon leaves a few bright points: the diamond ring. During totality (typically about two minutes), the sky is dark, bright stars and planets are visible, and the dim corona appears.
                        • The Moon’s distance varies, so its angular size varies a little. If the Moon is too small to cover the whole Sun: an annular eclipse (a ring of Sun is seen).
                        • Because of tidal friction the Moon is receding. Tens of thousands of years from now its angular size will be too small and there will be no more total solar eclipses.
                        Think like the exam

                        Why total eclipses are possible at all: the Sun is about 400 times larger than the Moon but also about 400 times farther away, so both look about 0.5°. When the Moon is nearer (perigee) it looks a little bigger than the Sun: total. When farther (apogee) it looks smaller: annular.

                        Mid-Autumn Festival question (Assignment 2 Q4b): a solar eclipse needs a new moon, but Mid-Autumn is near full moon (the Chinese lunar month starts at new moon, so the 15th day is near full), so the day before it should have no solar eclipse. Argue from the phase.

                        Saturn question (Assignment 2 Q6): Titan (0.24°) is much bigger than the Sun (0.056°) as seen from Saturn, so there total eclipses are easy.

                        Practise

                        Why do we always see the same side of the Moon?
                        Its rotation period equals its orbital period (29.5 days relative to the Sun, synchronous rotation).
                        What are the maria? Are they water?
                        No. They are dark plains of solidified lava, called “seas” historically.
                        What phase is the Moon during a solar eclipse? During a lunar eclipse?
                        New moon for a solar eclipse; full moon for a lunar eclipse.
                        Why do we not get a solar eclipse every month?
                        The Moon’s orbit is tilted relative to the ecliptic, so at most new moons the Moon’s shadow passes north or south of the Earth.
                        Will total solar eclipses last for ever?
                        No. Tidal friction makes the Moon recede; in tens of thousands of years its angular size will be too small to cover the Sun.

                        In one breath: Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars are small, rocky, solid-surfaced worlds. They are built from similar stuff, yet they turned out completely different. The reasons are only three: how far from the Sun, how thick the atmosphere, and how the planet is tilted and spun.

                          Mercury: the baked, cratered rock

                          From the notes
                          • Extremely thin atmosphere, mainly sodium and a little helium. Pressure almost zero.
                          • Gaseous sodium means it is hot enough to boil sodium out of the rock (it is the closest planet to the Sun).
                          • Huge difference between day and night temperature.
                          • Surface covered with impact craters (made by meteorites), with ray systems and central mountains.

                          Why the huge day–night swing? Think of a sunny beach with no wind and a clear sky: hot at noon, freezing at midnight. An atmosphere is a blanket that holds heat and spreads it around. Mercury has no blanket, so the sunlit side bakes and the dark side loses all its heat into space.

                          Why so many craters? Space is full of rocks. With no atmosphere to burn them up and no oceans or weather to erase the scars, every impact stays. A crater is a permanent record.

                          Think like the exam

                          A crater-count tells age. A surface with many craters has been exposed for a long time; a surface with few has been renewed or protected. Keep this idea: it explains Venus (protected), Earth (erased by weather and plates) and Mars (some old, some resurfaced by lava) too.

                          Mercury’s “day” is not what you think. It spins once in 59 days but orbits in 88 days. Use the calculator in section 3 to see that one Mercury sunrise-to-sunrise lasts about 176 Earth days, exactly two Mercury years.

                          Venus: the runaway greenhouse

                          From the notes
                          • Spins backwards (opposite to most planets; only Uranus is also like this), once in 243 days.
                          • Axis almost perpendicular to its orbit (Earth’s axis is tilted 23.5°), so no seasons.
                          • Atmosphere: pressure 90 times Earth’s; about 90% CO₂, 3% N₂, some SO₂.
                          • Completely covered by clouds of sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄): acid rain.
                          • Serious greenhouse effect: surface almost 500 °C, nearly the same everywhere.
                          • Acid rain dissolves carbonate and sulphate rocks and releases more CO₂ and SO₂, which strengthens the greenhouse effect further.
                          • Fewer craters than Mercury, thanks to protection from the thick atmosphere.

                          The greenhouse effect, in one picture

                          Sunlight passes through the atmosphere and warms the ground. The warm ground sends the energy back out as invisible heat (infrared). CO₂ absorbs that heat and sends part of it back down, like a blanket. The thicker the CO₂ blanket, the longer each bit of heat is stuck. Switch planets and watch the red dots.

                          Trap

                          Venus is hotter than Mercury even though it is almost twice as far from the Sun. Distance is not the whole story: Mercury has no atmosphere to trap heat. Whenever a question gives you a surprising temperature order, look for the atmosphere.

                          Venus spins backwards: what does that do to the day?

                          There are two kinds of “day”. The sidereal day is the time to spin once relative to the stars. The solar day is sunrise to sunrise. They differ because the planet also moves round the Sun. On Earth the planet must turn a little extra to face the Sun again (that is why a solar day is 4 minutes longer than a sidereal day). If the planet spins the opposite way to its orbit, the Sun comes back early, so the solar day is shorter.

                          same direction: 1/solar = 1/sidereal − 1/year
                          opposite direction: 1/solar = 1/sidereal + 1/year
                          Think like the exam

                          This is exactly Assignment 1 Question 7. “If the Earth’s rotation were reversed, would the sidereal day be longer than the solar day?” Yes: with reversed spin, the solar day gets shorter than the sidereal day, so the sidereal day is longer. Venus is the real-life version: spin 243 days backwards, year 224 days, so sunrise to sunrise takes only about 117 Earth days.

                          And on Venus the Sun rises in the west, because the spin is backwards. Seen from above, planets turn anticlockwise and the Sun rises in the east. Venus turns clockwise.

                          Mars: cold, red and volcanic

                          From the notes
                          • Axis tilted 24° (like Earth’s 23.5°) → seasons.
                          • Thin atmosphere: pressure only 1% of Earth’s; 95% CO₂, 3% N₂. Clouds of water vapour/CO₂ (white) and dust (yellowish).
                          • Too thin to trap heat → surface temperature swings from −100 °C to −10 °C. Also cold on average because it is far from the Sun.
                          • Red colour: iron oxide (rust) on the surface. Polar ice caps of CO₂ and water.
                          • Olympus Mons: largest volcano in the solar system, 25 km high (3× Mt Everest), 600 km across. No active volcanoes now.
                          • Canyon: 5000 km long, 200 km wide, 7 km deep (4.4× Grand Canyon).
                          • Giant volcanoes because of the absence of continental drift; also no long mountain ranges.
                          • Two small moons, the larger has radius under 13 km; not round.

                          Axis tilt makes the seasons

                          Seasons come from tilt, not from distance. When the north end of the axis leans toward the Sun, the Sun climbs high in the northern sky: summer. Half an orbit later it leans away: winter. If the axis is not tilted at all (Venus), the Sun never moves north or south: no seasons.

                          Why did Mars end up so different from Earth?

                          In the early solar system, Earth, Venus and Mars all had atmospheres of volcanic gases: mostly CO₂ and water vapour. So they started alike. On Earth the CO₂ was then mostly removed: it dissolved in the oceans and was used by plants in photosynthesis. Venus and Mars did not have those processes, so their atmospheres stayed CO₂-rich. Mars is small and far from the Sun, so its atmosphere is thin and cold; Venus is close and kept a thick atmosphere.

                          Think like the exam

                          “Why does Mars have bigger volcanoes than Earth?” The notes say: no continental drift. Here is the logic behind it. A volcano forms over a hot spot below the crust. On Earth the crust slides over the hot spot, so the lava builds a chain of modest volcanoes. On Mars the crust stays still, so the lava keeps piling up on the same spot for a very long time and builds one giant. Same reason Mars has no long mountain ranges: those come from plates colliding.

                          “Why is Mars’s temperature range large but Venus’s almost zero?” Thick atmosphere (Venus) spreads and stores heat so day equals night; a thin one (Mars) cannot, so it swings wildly. Mercury (no atmosphere) swings the most.

                          “Why can the Sun be overhead on Mars as on Earth?” Same tilt (24° vs 23.5°), so the Sun can be directly overhead only between about 24°N and 24°S: tropics for both.

                            • Very similar to Jupiter: fast spin, internal heating, atmosphere, metallic hydrogen core, strong magnetic field.
                            • Rings: many thin ringlets, only about 1 km thick; made of tiny dust and ice, millimetres to metres across. Formed when Saturn’s great tidal force tore one or more satellites into small parts.
                            • Gaps between ringlets (the obvious one: Cassini division). Shepherd satellites sit just inside and outside some ringlets and keep them stable and narrow.
                            • Titan: nitrogen atmosphere. Cassini-Huygens arrived 2004–05; the Huygens probe landed on Titan. Rivers and lakes of organic material.

                            Why does a tidal force tear things apart? The pull of gravity gets weaker with distance. So the near side of a moon is pulled harder than its far side. That difference stretches the moon. Too close to a heavy planet and the stretch beats the moon’s own gravity holding it together. This is the same idea as the Moon raising tides on Earth (Chapter 6).

                            Small objects in the solar system

                            In one breath: Besides the Sun and eight planets, the solar system holds dwarf planets (Pluto, Eris), comets (icy, with tails), asteroids (rocky, between Mars and Jupiter), Kuiper belt objects (icy-rocky, beyond Neptune) and meteoroids (grains and rocks that make shooting stars).

                            Lecture notes: Chapter 10

                              Pluto and the dwarf planets

                              From the notes: Pluto
                              • Neither terrestrial nor Jovian. New Horizons flew past in July 2015 (data trickled back at about 120 bytes per second, taking more than a year).
                              • Mass < 1% of Earth’s; diameter about 2370 km, smaller than the Moon (3475 km). Density about 2.3 (Jovian < 2, terrestrial > 5).
                              • Orbit inclined 17.2° to the ecliptic, and part of it lies inside Neptune’s. From 1979 to 1999 Neptune was actually the farthest planet.

                              • Five satellites. Largest = Charon, diameter 1200 km (over half of Pluto’s). Charon’s orbital period equals Pluto’s rotation period: a synchronous satellite.
                              From the notes: Eris

                              Discovered in 2005. One of the largest dwarf planets, diameter about 2336 km; the most massive Kuiper belt object found so far.

                              Why is Pluto a “dwarf planet”?

                              In 2006 the International Astronomical Union (IAU) decided that being round and orbiting the Sun is not enough to be a planet. A planet must also have cleared its neighbourhood: it is by far the biggest thing in its orbit, so everything else has been pulled in, thrown out or made into moons. Pluto shares its zone with many other Kuiper belt objects, so it has not cleared it. That makes it a dwarf planet. This is Assignment 2 Question 7.

                              Dwarf planet, in four lines (write this in the exam)
                              1. It orbits the Sun.
                              2. It has enough mass for its own gravity to make it (nearly) round.
                              3. It has not cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit.
                              4. It is not a satellite of another body.

                              Comets: dirty snowballs with tails

                              From the notes
                              • Modelled as “dirty snowballs”: a tiny nucleus (under 10 km) of frozen gases and dust.
                              • Nearing the Sun, gases vaporise and form an extended coma. Closer still, the solar wind (charged particles from the Sun) and the Sun’s radiation pressure push dust and gas away, making a long tail. Hence the tail always points away from the Sun.
                              • Closed (elliptical) orbits, visiting again and again. Halley: period 76 years. Most comets have average distances beyond Pluto’s.
                              • Rosetta mission: probe Philae landed on comet 67P in November 2014.
                              Think like the exam

                              Which way does the tail point when the comet is moving away from the Sun? Still away from the Sun, so it points ahead of the comet. The tail has nothing to do with the direction of motion.

                              Where is a comet brightest and most active? Near the Sun (perihelion), where it is warmest. Far away it is just a dark, frozen nucleus with no coma and no tail.

                              Use Kepler’s third law on comets. Period P (years) and average distance a (AU) satisfy P² = a³. Halley: a = 762/3 ≈ 18 AU. If it comes as close as 0.59 AU, the farthest point is 2 × 18 − 0.59 ≈ 35 AU, past Neptune. Try it, and try Sedna (76 to 900 AU).

                              The Sun

                              In one breath: The Sun is a huge ball of mostly hydrogen gas held together by its own gravity. In its core, hydrogen nuclei fuse into helium and release energy. That energy leaks out through the layers, and the outward push it creates balances gravity, which keeps the Sun stable for billions of years.

                              Lecture notes: Chapter 11

                                Atoms: the five facts you need

                                From the notes
                                • An atom is about 10⁻¹⁰ m across. Electrons orbit a nucleus about 10⁻¹⁴ m across that holds almost all the mass.
                                • The nucleus has protons (positive) and neutrons (neutral); they have about the same mass.

                                • By mass about 70% of the Sun is hydrogen; most of the rest is ⁴He. Hydrogen is the fuel, helium the product, but most of the helium was already there when the Sun formed.

                                Sunspots and solar activity

                                From the notes
                                • Granulation: hot gas rises, releases energy, cools, darkens and sinks. Each granule about 1/10 of Earth’s size, lasting about 20 minutes.
                                • Sunspots: dark regions, about 4000 K against 6000 K elsewhere. Comparable to Earth in size, usually in groups, living a few days to three weeks.
                                • The number of sunspots follows a cycle of about 11 years. At the start they appear at high latitudes; later they increase in number and appear at lower latitudes. Plot position against time: the butterfly diagram.
                                • Sunspots have a strong magnetic field (about 1000 times the Sun’s average) and come in pairs of opposite polarity, joined by field lines. The field locks the gas in place and stops the hotter gas from below rising, so they are cooler.
                                • Prominences: eruptions of gas trapped by magnetic fields in the chromosphere. Flares: far more violent eruptions emitting X-rays, ultraviolet, visible light and solar wind.
                                • Solar wind: mainly protons and electrons from the corona; strong wind gives aurora on Earth.
                                • Differential rotation: the Sun’s equator rotates faster than its poles. We believe sunspots and activity are due to this.
                                Think like the exam

                                The notes ask: “Do you know how to detect the differential rotation?” Track sunspots day by day. A spot near the equator crosses the face of the Sun faster (about 25 days for a full turn) than one near a pole (about 35 days). Another way: the Doppler shift of spectral lines at the east and west edges (Chapter 5) at different latitudes.

                                Sunspots are not “dark”. At 4000 K a spot is still very bright. It looks black only by contrast with the 6000 K around it. Cooler gas gives less light per area (Chapter 5).

                                Cause chain to learn: differential rotation → magnetic field twisted → sunspot pairs → prominences and flares → more solar wind → aurora on Earth.

                                Inside: making energy and getting it out

                                From the notes: energy production
                                • Chemical, gravitational and similar sources cannot last long enough. The Sun’s energy comes from nuclear fusion, the p-p chain: 4 ¹H → ⁴He + photons + others (neutrinos among the others: massless and neutral).
                                • Fusion needs high temperature (to overcome the electric repulsion between protons) and high density (to make collisions likely). Only in the core: over 10⁷ K.
                                From the notes: energy transport
                                • Radiative zone: a gamma-ray photon goes about 1 cm or less before being absorbed and re-emitted in a random direction, as one or more lower-energy photons. It takes about ten million years for the energy to reach the outer part, now as thousands of low-energy photons, mostly visible light.
                                • Convective zone: in the cooler, opaque outer gas radiation is not efficient, so convection carries the energy to the surface.
                                Think like the exam

                                Why does the energy take ten million years but light needs only 2.3 seconds to cross the Sun? Light in empty space goes straight. Inside the Sun the photon is absorbed and re-emitted again and again in random directions, so its path is a zig-zag. Distance actually travelled is enormously longer than the radius.

                                Energy bookkeeping: one gamma-ray photon becomes thousands of visible photons. The total energy is the same; it is just shared among more photons, each with less energy (longer wavelength). That is how gamma rays made in the core leave as mostly visible light.

                                Why can’t we copy the Sun on Earth easily? The notes: the extreme conditions (very high temperature and density) cannot yet be reproduced stably in a laboratory.

                                Stability: gravity against pressure

                                From the notes
                                • A forming star: gravity pulls gas together until the centre is hot and dense enough to ignite fusion.
                                • The energy made creates outward pressure (gas pressure from the hot material, and radiation pressure from photons) that balances the inward gravity. The star is stable for millions to billions of years.
                                • Both pressures come from the energy made in the core: no energy, the star collapses.
                                Think like the exam

                                Two forces, one answer. Whenever a question asks “what keeps the Sun stable?”, answer with both forces: gravity inward, pressure (gas + radiation) outward, equal. “If the core makes more energy, pressure rises and the star expands; if it makes none, the star collapses.”

                                Why is the Sun’s stability important to us? A steady output for billions of years let life develop on Earth.

                                Safe (from the notes)

                                Unsafe, never use
                                • Naked eye, directly.
                                • Sunglasses, exposed film, smoke-darkened glass, or a small filter near the eyepiece.
                                • Looking at a reflection in a pool of water or dark ink.

                                  • Sun: apparent −26.8, absolute 4.8. The same idea is used for clusters and galaxies.

At distance d its brightness is smaller by the factor (d/10)², which makes it dimmer by 2.5 log10((d/10)²) = 5 log10(d/10) magnitudes. So m = M + 5 log10(d/10 pc). Check with the Sun: 1 AU is 1/206,265 pc, so m = 4.8 + 5 log10(1/2,062,650) = 4.8 − 31.6 = −26.8. ✓

                                  • If d = 10 pc → m = M.
                                  • If d > 10 pc → m > M (the star looks dimmer than its true brightness).
                                  • If d < 10 pc → m < M.

                                  Compare real power: two stars with M₁ and M₂: L₁/L₂ = 100.4(M₂−M₁). The star with the smaller M is more luminous.

                                  Think like the exam: Assignment 2 Question 10

                                  “Star X has parallax 0.023″ and visual magnitude 9.4. (a) Find its absolute magnitude. (b) Does the Sun emit more energy than X?”

                                  1. d = 1/p = 1/0.023 = 43.5 pc.
                                  2. M = m − 5 log10(d/10) = 9.4 − 5 log10(4.35) = 9.4 − 3.19 = 6.2.
                                  3. (b) The Sun has M = 4.8, smaller than 6.2, so the Sun is more luminous: LSun/LX = 100.4×1.4 ≈ 3.7. Yes, the Sun emits about 3.7 times more energy.

                                  Always compare absolute magnitudes, never apparent ones, when the question is about how much energy a star emits. X looks much dimmer (9.4 vs −26.8) mainly because it is far away.

                                  Stellar distances: AU, light-year, parsec, parallax

                                  From the notes
                                  • Metres are too small. Planets: the astronomical unit (1 AU = Earth–Sun distance). Stars: the light-year, the distance light travels in one year. A light-year is a distance, not a time.
                                  • Parallax: as Earth orbits the Sun, a nearby star seems to move against the very distant background stars. Compare photos taken six months apart to measure the angle p. With p and 1 AU, simple trigonometry gives the distance d.
                                  • If p = 1 arcsecond, then d = 1 parsec ≈ 3.26 ly. (So d in pc = 1/p in arcseconds.) Gaia has measured about a billion stars. For larger distances other methods are needed.
                                  • Proper motion (unit: angle per year) is the star’s real motion across the sky. It depends on: (1) the speed of the real motion, (2) the distance, (3) the angle between the motion and our line of sight. A fast, far star can have the same proper motion as a slow, near one.
                                  Think like the exam: Assignment 2 Question 9

                                  “A star is 5 light years away. How far in parsecs? What is its parallax?”

                                  1. d = 5 ly ÷ 3.26 ly/pc = 1.53 pc.
                                  2. p = 1/d = 1/1.53 = 0.65 arcseconds.

                                  Sense check: nearer than 1 pc would give a parallax bigger than 1″; 1.5 pc is a bit farther, so a bit smaller than 1″. ✓

                                  Parallax vs proper motion, a favourite “compare” question. Parallax: apparent, back and forth, repeats each year, caused by Earth’s motion, used for distance. Proper motion: real, steady drift, adds up year after year, caused by the star’s motion, depends on speed and distance and direction.

                                  The Hertzsprung–Russell diagram

                                  From the notes
                                  • H-R diagram: plot of surface temperature against luminosity (in practice, absolute magnitude).
                                  • Spectral types O B A F G K M: hottest to coolest. Mnemonic: “Oh, Be A Fine Girl, Kiss Me”. By convention O (hottest) is plotted at the left.
                                  • Main sequence: the diagonal band from upper left to lower right; most stars are here.
                                  • Giants: upper right: low surface temperature (little energy per area) but high luminosity, so physically very large. Dwarfs: lower left: physically very small.
                                  Think like the exam

                                  Same temperature, different brightness → different size. Luminosity depends on temperature and surface area (Chapter 5). Two stars at the same temperature give the same light per square metre, so the more luminous one must have the larger surface. If star A is 100 times as luminous as B at the same temperature, A’s surface area is 100 times larger, so its radius is √100 = 10 times larger.

                                  Same luminosity, different temperature. A cool star that is as luminous as a hot one must be bigger (it needs a larger surface to give out the same total energy). That is why red giants are huge.

                                  Reading the diagram. Position on the H-R diagram describes the star’s physical properties (temperature, luminosity), not where it is in the sky.

                                  Colour and temperature. Blue stars are hot (O, B), white are A–F, yellow G (the Sun), orange K, red M. This follows from the black body idea (Chapter 5): the peak of the spectrum moves toward blue as the temperature rises.

                                  Double stars, variable stars, clusters and nebulae

                                  In one breath: Our Sun is a lonely, steady star, but over half of all stars belong to pairs or groups, many stars change brightness, many are packed in clusters, and the birthplaces of stars are glowing clouds called nebulae. These objects let us weigh stars and measure huge distances.

                                  Lecture notes: Chapter 13

                                    Binary stars: five ways to find a pair

                                    From the notes
                                    • Two stars orbiting each other under mutual gravity = binary (double) star; three or more = multiple star. Over 50% of stars are in binary or multiple systems.
                                    • Five types, named by how they are discovered, so one system can be several types:
                                      • Visual: both stars can be seen. Beware optical doubles (e.g. Mizar and Alcor): not bound, at different distances.
                                      • Spectroscopic: cannot be resolved, but the Doppler effect from orbital motion shows in the spectrum.
                                      • Eclipsing: one star passes in front of the other (special case of visual); it is a variable star (e.g. Algol).
                                      • Contact: so close they exchange material and may be deformed and glued together.
                                      • Astrometric: one star not observable; the visible one moves in a wavy line (how Sirius’s companion was found).
                                    • From the periods of revolution we can find the masses and sizes of the stars: very difficult for single stars.
                                    Think like the exam

                                    Match the evidence to the type. A question will describe an observation; you name the type: “two separate dots in a telescope” → visual; “spectral lines splitting and merging periodically” → spectroscopic; “brightness dips repeating every few days” → eclipsing (and so a variable); “a star wobbling along a wavy path with no visible partner” → astrometric; “gas bridge between the stars” → contact.

                                    Which star moves more? Both orbit the centre of mass, which lies closer to the heavier star. So the lighter star moves on the bigger circle, faster, and the heavier star on the smaller circle. Equal masses → same size circles. (Try the mass slider.)

                                    Why don’t all binaries eclipse? It depends on the inclination of the orbit: we need to see it nearly edge-on. A face-on orbit never gives an eclipse.

                                    Why binaries matter: weighing stars. The faster and closer the orbit, the heavier the stars (gravity must hold them), which is how masses are found.

                                    Bonus (not in the slides; use only if a question gives you the numbers): with the orbit’s average separation a in AU and period P in years, the total mass in Sun-masses is M = a³ ÷ P². Check with Earth and Sun: a = 1, P = 1 → 1 Sun-mass ✓.

                                    Variable stars and the cosmic distance ladder

                                    From the notes
                                    • Variable stars: brightness changes with time. The graph of brightness against time is the light curve.
                                    • Eclipsing variables: eclipsing binaries; the light curve is periodic. Only if the orbit is seen edge-on enough.
                                    • Pulsating variables: variable because of their internal structure: they keep expanding and contracting. The most famous: Cepheid variables, periods from a few days to a few months. Longer period → brighter (more luminous) star.
                                    • Measure the period → know the absolute magnitude → compare with the apparent magnitude → get the distance.
                                    • The period–luminosity relation is calibrated using nearby Cepheids whose distances are known by parallax (Henrietta Swan Leavitt). In general, accurate indicators for short distances calibrate indicators for greater distances: the cosmic distance ladder. Cepheids are the foundation of this ladder.
                                    • Eruptive variables: sudden changes. Novae are usually binaries: material moves from one star to the other, and an explosion happens on or near the surface of the receiving star once enough has piled up. (Supernovae: Chapter 16.)
                                    1. Rung 1: parallax measures nearby stars directly (a few hundred pc at best from the ground; more with Gaia).
                                    2. Rung 2: Cepheids, calibrated by those nearby parallax stars, reach much farther.
                                    3. Further rungs (other indicators) are calibrated by the Cepheids and reach even farther.
                                    Think like the exam

                                    “Explain how a Cepheid can be used to find the distance to a galaxy.” Write the four-step chain: (1) measure the period from the light curve; (2) the period–luminosity relation gives the absolute magnitude; (3) measure the average apparent magnitude; (4) m − M gives the distance (m − M = 5 log₁₀(d/10 pc)). The most common lost mark: forgetting why the relation can be trusted: it was calibrated with nearby Cepheids of known (parallax) distance.

                                    Eclipsing vs pulsating. Eclipsing: the star itself is steady, the brightness changes because of geometry (one hides the other). Pulsating: the star itself swells and shrinks. Eruptive: sudden outbursts.

                                    Reading an eclipsing light curve (reasoning, not in the notes). The brightness dips each time one star hides the other. The deeper dip happens when the hotter, brighter star is hidden; the time between two similar dips is the orbital period (or half of it, if the stars are similar).

                                    Nebulae: the cradles of stars

                                    From the notes
                                    • Typical space has about 1 atom per cm³; in a nebula, over 1000 atoms/cm³ (still about the best vacuum humans can make; air is about 10¹⁹ atoms/cm³).

                                    • Nebulae are the birthplaces of stars. Don’t confuse them with planetary nebulae (Chapter 15).
                                    Think like the exam

                                    Why call a nebula “dense” if it is a better vacuum than anything on Earth? Because it is dense compared with the surrounding space (1000 vs 1 atom/cm³), and over a hundred light-years that adds up to enough gas to make many stars. “Dense” in astronomy is always relative.

                                    Practise: questions the exam might ask

                                    Give two reasons why eclipsing binaries are useful to astronomers.

                                    What is the difference between an open cluster and a multiple star system?
                                    A multiple star system has only 3 or a few stars orbiting each other; a cluster has tens to millions of stars bound by gravity. Cluster stars formed together, are about the same age and distance.
                                    Two stars appear to be next to each other in the sky. How do you decide whether they are a true binary or an optical double?
                                    Measure their distances (for example by parallax) and see if they are about the same; check for orbital motion or for a common proper motion over the years. Optical doubles are at very different distances and not gravitationally bound (Mizar and Alcor).
                                    Why would a nova happen in a binary but not in a single star?
                                    A nova needs material transferred from one star to its companion. When enough has accumulated on the receiving star’s surface, it explodes. A single star has no such supply.

                                    Birth of stars

                                    In one breath: A cold cloud of gas and dust is disturbed, contracts under its own gravity, heats up as a protostar, and, if it has at least about 0.1 solar mass, ignites hydrogen fusion and settles onto the main sequence. Mass decides everything: how fast it forms, what kind of star it is, how it makes energy, and how long it lives.

                                    Lecture notes: Chapter 14

                                      Why we need the H-R diagram to study star life

                                      From the notes

                                      A human lifetime is far too short to watch a star change. So we study many stars at different stages and deduce the time scales: the H-R diagram (absolute magnitude against surface temperature / spectral type) does this. It shows that most stars spend about 90% of their lifetime on the main sequence.

                                      Think of a forest: you cannot watch one tree grow for 500 years, but a walk through the forest shows seedlings, young trees and old trees. The H-R diagram is the forest photo.

                                      Making a star: from cloud to main sequence

                                      From the notes
                                      • Interstellar clouds: gas and dust, mostly hydrogen, very low density; up to thousands of light-years across and 10 to 1000 solar masses. A visible one is a nebula (e.g. the Eagle nebula).
                                      • Undisturbed, they don’t change. Triggers: galaxy collisions, density waves, shock waves of a supernova, the birth of a nearby star.
                                      • Contraction forms a protostar: no nuclear reaction yet, so it is not a star.
                                      • Clumps of 10 to 30 solar masses shrink to about the size of the solar system in only 10,000 years or so and become O and B stars. Less massive ones form T Tauri stars (surrounded by a huge cloud, young, low surface temperature, most light in the infrared, look red), which become G, K or M stars.
                                      • General principle: a violent process emits short wavelengths (gamma, X-rays); a gentle one emits long wavelengths (infrared, radio).
                                      • Protostars have accretion disks and jets (along the rotation poles; last only about 100,000 years).
                                      • Slightly more massive than a planet but too small to burn fuel: brown dwarf (very dim). At about 0.1 solar mass or more, the centre gets hot and dense enough for fusion: a star is born. Its light and heat push away the gas and dust; the disk remains as the protoplanetary disk.
                                      • Gas pressure (fast-moving particles) plus radiation pressure push out against gravity: the star is stable: main sequence.
                                      Think like the exam

                                      “Is a protostar a star? Is a brown dwarf?” No to both: a star needs nuclear reactions in the core. A protostar has not started them yet; a brown dwarf never will (for hydrogen). They are, however, connected to Chapter 12’s definition: massive enough to have nuclear reactions in its core. Jupiter is under 0.1 solar mass (about 0.001), so it is far below the line.

                                      Order of events: disturbance → contraction → protostar (gravitational energy) → ignition at the centre (fusion) → pressure balances gravity → main sequence. If you are asked for the sequence, give these six words in order.

                                      Two energy sources, don’t mix them up: in the protostar stage, energy comes from gravitational energy (like a ball dropping, speeding up). On the main sequence, energy comes from nuclear fusion. Nuclear power plants on Earth use fission (splitting a large nucleus); stars use fusion (joining small nuclei).

                                      The path on the H-R diagram

                                      From the notes
                                      • The formation is shown on the H-R diagram by an evolutionary track. Mass is the most important factor that determines the fate of a star.
                                      • The cold, dim cloud is a point at the right. Contracting, it heats up and moves to the left. Luminosity per surface area rises with temperature. But if the star is not very massive, its luminosity drops, because its size decreases much faster.
                                      • It takes about 0.1 to 100 million years for the cloud to become a main-sequence star. After that it stays for millions to billions of years.
                                      • A star’s position on the H-R diagram shows its physical properties, not its place in the sky.
                                      Think like the exam

                                      Luminosity = surface temperature effect × size effect. While the cloud contracts the surface gets hotter (that makes each square metre brighter) but the area gets smaller (that lowers the total). For a low-mass star the shrinking wins, so it moves left and down. For a massive star the heating wins, so it moves left and up or level.

                                      Time to reach the main sequence: a 10–30 solar mass clump needs only about 10,000 years to shrink to solar-system size; lighter clumps need much longer (the range is 0.1 to 100 million years in total). More massive = faster everything.

                                      On the main sequence: mass decides everything

                                      From the notes
                                      • Energy now comes from fusion (several small nuclei combining into a large one).
                                      • Mass below about 1.1 solar masses (core under 15 million K): main reaction is the p-p chain (4 H → 1 He), the star has a radiative core and convective shell, like the Sun.
                                      • Mass above about 1.1 solar masses: the CNO cycle. Same net result as the p-p chain, with carbon as a catalyst. Carbon’s nucleus has 6 times the positive charge of hydrogen, so the reaction needs the higher temperature and density of more massive stars. The CNO cycle is faster and produces more energy per second. The core must be convective to carry the heat out; the outer part is hot and not too opaque, so radiation does the job: radiative shell and convective core.
                                      • The most massive stars use up their core fuel in about a million years; less massive stars can live tens of billions of years.

Lifetime = fuel ÷ rate, so lifetime shrinks as mass grows. Compare a huge bonfire that burns in an hour with a tiny candle that lasts all night.

                                      Why does a higher-mass star need the CNO cycle? Gravity squeezes a more massive star harder, so its core is hotter. Carbon’s charge is 6 times bigger than hydrogen’s, so the repulsion to overcome is larger and a hotter, denser core is needed. Hence “> 1.1 solar masses → CNO”.

                                      Which star lives longer: a 0.5 solar mass or a 5 solar mass star? The 0.5 one, by a huge factor (tens of billions of years against a few hundred million). And since 90% of its life is on the main sequence, you are more likely to see a long-lived star on the main sequence: most stars you can observe are low-mass main-sequence stars.

                                      Same net effect, different route: p-p chain and CNO cycle both turn 4 hydrogen into 1 helium. Don’t say the CNO cycle makes a different product.

                                      Describe the formation of a star from an interstellar cloud, in order.

                                      What is a brown dwarf, and how does it differ from a star and a planet?
                                      It is a protostar-like object slightly more massive than a planet, but below about 0.1 solar mass. It never gets hot enough to fuse hydrogen, so it is not a star, and it is very dim and hard to find. A planet is smaller still, and orbits a star.
                                      Why do T Tauri stars appear red?
                                      They are young, with low surface temperature, and shine mostly in the infrared. The notes’ general principle: gentle processes emit long wavelengths. They are surrounded by a huge cloud and look red.
                                      Compare a 0.5 solar mass and a 10 solar mass star on the main sequence.

                                      A student says: “The Sun will stay on the main sequence for ever because fusion keeps it stable.” Correct them.
                                      Fusion keeps it stable only while there is hydrogen fuel in the core. The Sun spends about 90% of its life on the main sequence, which is billions of years, not for ever. When the fuel runs out the star leaves the main sequence (Chapter 15).

                                      On the right (low temperature, dim). Contraction heats it, so it moves to the left toward the main sequence.

                                      Chapter 15

                                      Death of less massive stars

                                      In one breath: When a star like the Sun runs out of hydrogen in its core, the core shrinks and heats, hydrogen burns in a shell, and the star swells into a cool, luminous red giant. It then throws off its outer layers as a planetary nebula and its core becomes a white dwarf that cools for ever. Mass decides the whole story.

                                      Lecture notes: Chapter 15 · after Test 2

                                        Mass decides the lifetime

                                        From the notes
                                        • A star spends about 90% of its life on the main sequence fusing hydrogen into helium in the core.
                                        • Misconception: more mass means more fuel, so a longer life. In fact, the core of a massive star is hotter and denser, so it burns much faster and lives a shorter time.
                                        • Evidence from clusters: stars in a cluster were born together (same age) with different masses. In the H-R diagram of a cluster, the more massive stars (upper left) have already left the main sequence; the stars at the turn-off point are about to die. This also tells which cluster is older.
                                        • How long a star lives depends exclusively on its mass; so does how it dies.
                                        Think like the exam

                                        Two clusters on the same H-R diagram: the one whose main sequence ends lower down (at lower mass, redder, dimmer stars) is the older. A young open cluster still has bright blue stars; a globular cluster has none. “Why do massive stars die first?” is answered by the burn rate, not the fuel supply.

                                        The death of a Sun-like star

                                        From the notes
                                        • When no hydrogen is left in the core, the p-p chain stops. (Solar-mass stars use the p-p chain; stars over 1.1 solar masses use the CNO cycle.) The helium core collapses and heats the hydrogen envelope around it. Fusion begins in the envelope and the star expands.
                                        • The star becomes very big and its surface temperature decreases, although the core is still very hot and the total luminosity is high. Cool surface: the star looks red: a red giant. The Sun could become as large as the Earth’s orbit.
                                        • All red giants are variable. The core keeps shrinking and heating until the triple-alpha process (helium flash) starts: three helium nuclei fuse into one carbon nucleus. The hydrogen shell and helium core do not give out energy steadily, so the star pulsates and drives a strong stellar wind; the whole outer shell is ejected as a thin shell: a planetary nebula (the Ring Nebula, M57).
                                        • The core does not get hot enough to burn carbon. With no more burning, it shrinks, becomes fainter and hotter: a white dwarf. Electron degenerate pressure stops further contraction and needs no fuel, so it supports the star for ever.
                                        • A typical white dwarf is slightly smaller than the Earth with about the mass of the Sun; its density is about 300,000 times that of rock. After radiating all its remaining energy it would become a black dwarf, but this takes longer than the age of the universe, so none exists yet.
                                        • On the H-R diagram: the Sun moves right and up to the red giant region, becomes unstable and ejects a planetary nebula, then moves quickly to the lower left (white dwarf).

                                        Why red and luminous? Cool surface (low T) makes it red; but luminosity ∝ R²T⁴ and R is huge, so the total power is high (Chapter 5). That is why a red giant is at the upper right of the H-R diagram.

                                        Why does a star expand when its core fuel runs out? The inert helium core contracts and heats; this heat ignites hydrogen in the shell around it, and the energy pushes the outer layers outwards.

                                        Planetary nebulae have nothing to do with planets; the name is historical. White dwarfs are not stars in the strict sense (no fusion) but cooling stellar cores, held up by electron pressure, not heat.

                                        Low-mass stars and novae

                                        From the notes
                                        • A star below about 0.4 solar mass has an uneventful life: it quietly burns hydrogen to helium and becomes a white dwarf. (More massive stars: Chapters 16 and 17.)
                                        • Nova (binary systems): one star has become a white dwarf and its companion is becoming a red giant, very close, maybe only the Earth–Moon distance. When the red giant’s surface gravity becomes weaker than the white dwarf’s, matter flows to the white dwarf. When enough accumulates, nuclear reactions start on the surface for a short time. The system brightens by about 100,000 times: a nova. The explosion does not disturb the white dwarf, so the accretion and the outburst can repeat.
                                        Think like the exam

                                        Remember the contrast for later: a nova is a surface explosion and the white dwarf survives (repeats). A Type Ia supernova (Chapter 18) occurs when the white dwarf reaches 1.4 solar masses and is destroyed. A 100,000-fold brightening is 12.5 magnitudes: Δm = 2.5 log₁₀(10⁵) = 12.5.

                                        Practise

                                        A cluster has no blue main-sequence stars, but many red giants. Young or old?
                                        Old: its massive, hot, blue stars have already left the main sequence and its turn-off point is at low mass.
                                        Why does a white dwarf not collapse further although it has no fusion?
                                        Electron degenerate pressure supports it; it does not come from burning any fuel, so it lasts for ever.
                                        What will the Sun become? Put the steps in order.
                                        Main sequence → red giant (as big as Earth’s orbit) → helium burning and pulsations → planetary nebula → white dwarf → (eventually) black dwarf.
                                        Why is there no black dwarf yet?
                                        Cooling takes much longer than the age of the universe.

                                        Chapter 16

                                        Death of massive stars

                                        In one breath: A star over about 8 solar masses fuses heavier and heavier elements until its core is iron, which cannot release energy. The core collapses in a fraction of a second, rebounds, and the star blows apart as a Type II supernova. What is left depends on the core’s mass: white dwarf, neutron star (often a pulsar) or black hole.

                                        Lecture notes: Chapter 16 · after Test 2

                                          Red supergiants and supernovae

                                          From the notes
                                          • A main-sequence star above about 8 solar masses has a hotter, denser core, burns faster and is of spectral type O, B or A. It lives much less: a 15 solar mass star only about 10 million years.
                                          • After hydrogen burning, the core is hot enough for helium → carbon, with a hydrogen shell around it. The star swells larger than a red giant: a red supergiant, about 100 times larger than a red giant, cool but extremely luminous (absolute magnitude up to −12, against 4.8 for the Sun).
                                          • Carbon burning follows; light nuclei fuse into heavier and heavier elements until iron. The oxygen, nitrogen, silicon and so on on Earth were made in earlier stars. Iron is the dead end: fusing iron needs energy instead of releasing it (that is why heavy nuclei such as uranium can be split to release energy).
                                          • As iron accumulates, the core pressure falls. Within one hundredth of a second the inner core collapses and heats dramatically; electrons combine with protons to form neutrons and neutrinos. Neutrinos barely interact with matter, so the star is transparent to them, and they carry away most of the energy.
                                          • The inner core cannot be compressed beyond the upper limit of nuclear density, so it bounces. The outgoing core hits the incoming outer core: shock waves, formation of heavy elements like uranium, and the outer layers are thrown off: a Type II supernova.
                                          • The star brightens by up to 15 magnitudes. The last naked-eye supernova: SN1987A in the Large Magellanic Cloud (not visible from Hong Kong). In 1054 AD Chinese astronomers recorded a “guest star” in Taurus, visible in daytime for about two months; its remnant is the Crab Nebula (M1). Over a thousand supernovae are found every year, but in other galaxies and very dim.
                                          Think like the exam

                                          15 magnitudes means a factor 100.4 × 15 = 10⁶: a million times brighter, comparable to a whole galaxy. Why is iron the end? The binding energy per particle peaks at iron: fusing lighter nuclei releases energy, fusing iron absorbs it. “Why does the core collapse?” Because the fuel can no longer provide pressure to hold up the weight.

                                          What remains

                                          From the notes
                                          • It depends on the mass of the core left after the explosion: less than 1.4 solar masses → white dwarf; 1.4 to about 3 → neutron star; more → black hole.

                                          Neutron stars and pulsars

                                          From the notes
                                          • With a core between 1.4 and about 3 solar masses, electron degenerate pressure fails. Electrons are squeezed into nuclei and combine with protons to form neutrons; neutron degenerate pressure stops the collapse (and, like electron pressure, needs no fuel).
                                          • A neutron star is 95–99% neutrons, radius 8 to 16 km (about the size of Hong Kong Island). No hills can form because gravity is so strong. A solid crust about 1 km thick, a layer of liquid neutrons below, maybe a solid core.
                                          • Magnetic field 10⁸ to 10¹⁵ times the Earth’s. Spiralling electrons make radio waves along the magnetic poles. The rotation axis usually does not line up with the magnetic axis, so the beams sweep round like a lighthouse: we receive regular pulses of radio waves: a pulsar. One is at the centre of the Crab Nebula.
                                          • Pulsars were discovered in the 1960s and at first thought to be signals from other civilisations. Millisecond pulsars spin a few milliseconds per turn; the strong gravity of the neutron star keeps them from flying apart.
                                          • Periods are so regular (more than ten decimal places) that tiny disturbances can be detected: a planet orbiting a neutron star makes the pulses arrive slightly early or late. This is how the first extrasolar planet was found.
                                          • If the companion is a giant, matter flows onto the neutron star and X-rays are emitted: an X-ray pulsar.
                                          Think like the exam

                                          Typical applied questions: “Why do we see a pulsar only if the beam sweeps across the Earth?” (a lighthouse beam must point at you), “Why is a pulsar’s period so stable?” (huge mass, huge rotational inertia), “Why is a neutron star so small yet so heavy?” (degeneracy pressure of neutrons allows extreme density). Compare: white dwarf ~ Earth-sized; neutron star ~ 10 km; each held up by degeneracy pressure, not heat.

                                          Practise

                                          Why can’t the star release energy by fusing iron?
                                          Fusing iron into heavier nuclei needs energy rather than releasing it; iron is the dead end of nuclear fusion in a star.
                                          Why does the Type II supernova happen?
                                          Once the core is iron it loses pressure, collapses in about 0.01 s, bounces at nuclear density, and the shock throws off the outer layers.
                                          A supernova leaves a core of 2.2 solar masses. What forms?
                                          A neutron star (between 1.4 and about 3 solar masses).
                                          What carries most of the energy away during the collapse?
                                          Neutrinos: the star is transparent to them.
                                          What was the “guest star” of 1054 AD?
                                          A supernova in Taurus; its remnant is the Crab Nebula (M1), with a pulsar at its centre.

                                          Quick check

                                          Chapter 16 must-know list

                                          • Above about 8 M☉: red supergiant, fusion up to iron, collapse in 0.01 s, neutrinos, bounce, Type II supernova (+15 mag).
                                          • Core left: under 1.4 white dwarf; 1.4–3 neutron star; over 3 black hole.
                                          • Neutron star: 8–16 km, neutron degeneracy pressure, huge magnetic field, lighthouse pulsar, millisecond pulsars, first exoplanet, X-ray pulsar.
                                          • SN1987A (LMC), 1054 AD guest star → Crab Nebula.

                                          Chapter 17

                                          Black holes

                                          In one breath: If a collapsed core is heavier than about 3 solar masses, nothing known can hold it up, and it becomes a black hole: a region where gravity is so strong that the escape speed exceeds the speed of light. You cannot see one, but you can weigh it from the way things orbit it, and see the X-rays from gas falling in.

                                          Lecture notes: Chapter 17 · after Test 2

                                            What a black hole is

                                            From the notes
                                            • If the remnant is heavier than two or three solar masses, neutron degenerate pressure cannot support it, and there is no known stronger force: it collapses into a black hole.
                                            • Escape velocity: throw a rock faster than this and it never returns. It is larger for a smaller or heavier object. Einstein: nothing can go faster than light. So if the escape velocity at the surface exceeds the speed of light, nothing, not even light, can escape.
                                            • General relativity: mass affects the spacetime around it. The Moon orbits the Earth not because of a “pull” but because the Earth bends spacetime (like a marble on an uneven table). Light also bends near a massive object. A black hole bends light the most.
                                            • The boundary is the event horizon (outsiders cannot observe events inside). For a non-rotating, uncharged (Schwarzschild) black hole, its radius is RS = 2GM/c², which depends only on the mass; for the Sun’s mass it is about 3 km.
                                            • The centre is the singularity. Not “zero radius, infinite density”: we simply do not know the physical laws there.
                                            • Outside the horizon, a photon sphere lets light orbit in a closed loop. Far away, the effect falls off: if the Sun suddenly became a black hole, the planets’ orbits would not change at all.
                                            Think like the exam

                                            Scaling is everything: RS ∝ M. A 10 M☉ black hole has RS ≈ 30 km; the Earth’s would be about 9 mm. “Is a black hole a cosmic vacuum cleaner?” No: from far away it attracts exactly like any object of the same mass. Danger exists only very close.

                                            Falling in, and rotating black holes

                                            From the notes
                                            • Seen from far away: a falling person speeds up at first, then slows near the horizon, appears in slow motion and finally almost stationary: you never see them reach the horizon.
                                            • From the falling person’s view (if they survive): the black hole grows larger as they approach; after crossing the horizon it engulfs almost everything; they see the outside world through a small window behind them; no jolt at the horizon; soon they hit the singularity.
                                            • Rotating (Kerr) black holes have two horizons (outer and inner). Between them is the ergosphere: things there are dragged round with the hole but can still escape. Inside the inner horizon nothing escapes. Rotational energy can be extracted by splitting an object so that one part falls in and the other comes out faster.

                                            Detecting black holes

                                            From the notes
                                            • We cannot see them, but we can find something dark and dense. Kepler’s laws still hold outside the horizon: the orbital speed of gas around the object (measured by the Doppler effect) gives a lower limit on its mass. If it is over 3 solar masses, in a small space (not a cluster), and not emitting much light: very likely a black hole.
                                            • A real black hole usually has an accretion disk and jets from the poles. Infalling matter gives out intense X-rays, a sign of a black hole (or possibly a neutron star).
                                            • Cygnus X-1: oldest black hole candidate; a dark companion of a type O star; lower mass limit 7 solar masses; X-ray source.
                                            Think like the exam

                                            The logic chain is the exam answer: (1) measure the orbital speed and radius (Doppler + Kepler); (2) mass above the maximum for a neutron star (about 3 M☉); (3) it is dark and compact; (4) X-rays from an accretion disk ⇒ black hole. The mass is a lower limit because the orbit’s tilt is unknown.

                                            Supermassive and advanced topics

                                            From the notes
                                            • There is no intrinsic mass limit on a black hole. Supermassive black holes are found at the centres of some galaxies. M87’s has 6.5 × 10⁹ solar masses (imaged by the Event Horizon Telescope in 2019). NGC 4261: 1.2 × 10⁹ solar masses within a volume about the size of the solar system.
                                            • No-hair theorem: only mass, angular momentum (spin) and electric charge can be known about a black hole; two with the same values behave identically, regardless of what formed them. Real ones are usually neutral.
                                            • Hawking radiation: black holes radiate with a black-body spectrum; the temperature is inversely proportional to the mass, extremely low for stellar ones (about 6 × 10⁻⁸ K for the Sun’s mass).
                                            • Mini black holes: no mechanism produces them after the first second of the universe, but they are theoretically possible. The end of a radiating black hole that loses all its mass is an open research question.
                                            • Worm holes exist in theory; but both ends look like black holes from outside, and you would be crushed by the singularity before getting out.
                                            Think like the exam

                                            Hawking temperature ∝ 1/M: a 10 M☉ hole is 10 times colder (6 × 10⁻⁹ K), colder than the 2.7 K background, so it absorbs more than it radiates. Compare formation routes: stellar black holes come from collapsing cores (over 3 M☉); supermassive ones are at galaxy centres (Chapter 18).

                                            Practise

                                            Why can’t light escape from a black hole?
                                            The escape velocity at the event horizon is greater than the speed of light, and nothing can travel faster than light.
                                            Find the Schwarzschild radius of a 5 solar mass black hole.
                                            RS ≈ 3 km × 5 = 15 km (about 14.8 km).
                                            Gas orbits an invisible X-ray source at 150 km/s, radius 0.4 AU. What mass?
                                            M ≈ (150/29.78)² × 0.4 = 10 M☉, a black hole candidate.
                                            Does the Earth’s orbit change if the Sun becomes a black hole of the same mass?
                                            No: gravity at a distance depends only on the mass.
                                            What is inside the event horizon of a black hole?
                                            The region the black hole occupies; its centre is the singularity, where the known laws fail.

                                            Quick check

                                            Chapter 17 must-know list

                                            • Escape speed above c → black hole. Event horizon; RS = 2GM/c² (3 km for the Sun); singularity (laws unknown); photon sphere; far-away orbits unchanged.
                                            • Falling in: outsider sees freezing at the horizon; faller feels no jolt. Kerr: two horizons, ergosphere.
                                            • Detection: orbital speed → lower mass limit (over 3 M☉), compact, dark, X-rays; Cygnus X-1 (7 M☉). Supermassive: M87, NGC 4261.
                                            • No-hair theorem; Hawking radiation (T ∝ 1/M); mini black holes; worm holes.

                                            Chapter 18

                                            Galaxies

                                            In one breath: Galaxies are the building blocks of the visible universe. Our Milky Way is a fairly typical barred spiral with about 200 billion stars; its outer stars orbit too fast for the visible matter, so dark matter must be there. There are about 100 billion galaxies of three types (elliptical, spiral, irregular), measured with Cepheids and Type Ia supernovae, and they collide and sometimes power quasars.

                                            Lecture notes: Chapter 18 · after Test 2

                                              The Milky Way Galaxy

                                              From the notes
                                              • Almost all naked-eye stars belong to the Milky Way: about 200 billion stars, mostly in the galactic disk: diameter about 100,000 ly, thickness only 1000 ly.
                                              • At the centre is the nuclear bulge (possibly with a bar), radius about 20,000 ly. A spherical halo surrounds the disk and bulge; it contains old stars, some in globular clusters, and interstellar matter.
                                              • Spiral arms hold young stars. By the laws of mechanics they should wind up and vanish (outer stars orbit more slowly). The accepted explanation is the density wave theory: stars orbit at different speeds, but the interstellar medium forms a density wave; at the wave front the density is higher and star formation is enhanced. Heavy, bright young stars die soon and cannot travel far, so the arms stay bright.
                                              • The Sun is in the Orion arm, about two-thirds of the way out from the centre. Arms are hard to map because we are inside the galaxy and dust lanes block the view. 21 cm radio emission from hydrogen and radio emission from molecular gas clouds trace the arms.
                                              • Seen from Earth the galaxy is a band of stars; the centre is toward Sagittarius, a summer constellation, so the Milky Way is most spectacular in summer, from a dark site.
                                              Think like the exam

                                              Use ratios to feel the shape: the disk is 100 times wider than thick (like a CD). Typical applied questions: “Why are young stars in the arms and old stars in the halo?” (star formation happens in dense gas in the disk; halo stars are the oldest), “Why 21 cm?” (hydrogen emits radio waves that dust does not block).

                                              Dark matter

                                              From the notes
                                              • Our galaxy also holds objects that emit no light, dark matter, revealed by gravity. How fast a star orbits depends on how much matter lies inside its orbit. If all matter were visible, orbital speeds near the edge should fall; instead they stay high, so there is more matter than we see. Dark matter is believed to be everywhere; we do not understand its nature.
                                              • Gravitational lensing: mass bends light. If a galaxy lies right behind another, the far galaxy’s image becomes a ring (in practice, arcs). The ring size tells the total mass of the middle galaxy; comparing it with the visible mass gives the dark matter.
                                              Think like the exam

                                              Argument pattern: speed depends on mass inside the orbit → observed speed too high → extra mass → dark matter. Same method as weighing a black hole (Chapter 17) and a binary star (Chapter 13).

                                              Classification of galaxies

                                              From the notes
                                              • About 100 billion galaxies. Gas and dust in our own galaxy hide some, but the distribution is fairly uniform.
                                              • Elliptical (e.g. M87): mostly old stars; some dwarf ellipticals have only 10 million stars, not unlike a large globular cluster.
                                              • Spiral: young and old stars; generally brighter than ellipticals; normal or barred (elongated bulge). M31 (Andromeda) is a normal spiral 2,000,000 ly away. The Milky Way is probably barred.
                                              • Irregular: no regular shape; believed to be the most common. The Large and Small Magellanic Clouds are two satellite galaxies of ours, visible to the naked eye but not from Hong Kong.
                                              • Hubble’s “tuning fork” diagram has sub-classes, but it is not an evolution track.

                                              Distances to galaxies

                                              From the notes
                                              • Before the 1920s galaxies were thought to be nearby “spiral nebulae” inside our galaxy. Today two main methods:
                                              • Cepheid variables (Chapter 13): period gives luminosity.
                                              • Type Ia supernovae: a white dwarf in a binary with a red giant gains mass until it reaches 1.4 solar masses, then electron degenerate pressure fails and it collapses and brightens. All have the same mass, so about the same luminosity: measure the apparent brightness, then compute the distance. Spectra and light curves distinguish the type.
                                              • Type Ia supernovae are much brighter than Cepheids, so they reach farther galaxies, but they are much rarer. Both are distance indicators.
                                              Think like the exam

                                              The method is always standard candle + apparent brightness → distance: m − M = 5 log₁₀(d/10 pc). Example (with M ≈ −19.3 as a typical Type Ia value): m = 19.7 gives m − M = 39, d = 10 × 107.8 pc ≈ 630 Mpc. Know why they differ: a nova (surface burst, repeatable) is not a Type Ia supernova (the white dwarf is destroyed) and is far dimmer.

                                              Interacting and active galaxies

                                              From the notes
                                              • Typical distance between galaxies is only 20–40 galaxy sizes (compare stars: tens of millions of star sizes), so collisions are common. Stars do not collide; the distribution of stars is distorted by gravity, streams of stars (“antennae”) are thrown out, the galaxies merge. Mergers also trigger star formation; multi-nucleus galaxies may be old mergers.
                                              • Active galaxy (active galactic nucleus, AGN): an unusually large energy output in infrared, ultraviolet and X-rays from the nucleus. The popular hypothesis: a supermassive black hole with infalling matter; collisions may create it.
                                              • Quasar (quasi-stellar object): looks small and dim like a star in visible light, bright in infrared and radio, with a large redshift (very far away: Chapter 19), so radiating more than nearby active galaxies. Probably the supermassive black hole at the centre of a young galaxy.
                                              Think like the exam

                                              Why can galaxies collide while stars do not? The space between stars is enormous (ratio of star separation to star size is in the tens of millions), but between galaxies it is only a few galaxy sizes.

                                              Practise

                                              Why do astronomers think dark matter exists in our galaxy?
                                              Stars near the edge orbit faster than the visible matter can explain, so there must be extra unseen mass inside their orbits. Lensing is another evidence.
                                              Which galaxy type contains mostly old stars? Which has young and old?
                                              Elliptical: mostly old. Spiral: both young and old.
                                              Why can a Type Ia supernova measure larger distances than a Cepheid?
                                              It is far brighter, so it can be seen in much more distant galaxies.
                                              Where is the Sun in the Milky Way?
                                              On the Orion arm, about two-thirds of the way from the centre.

                                              Chapter 18 must-know list

                                              • Milky Way: 200 billion stars; disk 100,000 ly × 1000 ly; bulge 20,000 ly; halo with globular clusters; arms by density wave; Sun in the Orion arm; 21 cm.

                                              • Distance indicators: Cepheids; Type Ia (1.4 M☉). Collisions common; AGN and quasars → supermassive black holes.

                                              Chapter 19

                                              Cosmology

                                              In one breath: The universe looks the same everywhere and in every direction on the large scale; it is filled with a 3 K microwave glow left over from a hot, dense beginning; its galaxies are rushing apart with speed proportional to distance (Hubble’s law); and winding that expansion backwards gives an age of about 14 billion years (the Big Bang). Its future depends on whether the total density is above or below the critical density.

                                              Lecture notes: Chapter 19 · after Test 2

                                                The cosmological principles

                                                From the notes
                                                • Universality: physical laws found near the Earth apply everywhere. (Otherwise no physical astronomy.)
                                                • Homogeneity: the distribution of matter does not vary with position. Locally it is wrong (the Sun holds most of the solar system’s mass; most Milky Way stars are in the disk). The Milky Way is in the Local Group (with M31, LMC, SMC). A cluster has about 1000 galaxies within a million parsecs; superclusters have about 100 clusters in 100 Mpc. The Local Group and the Virgo Cluster belong to the Local Supercluster. Larger structures (the Great Wall, voids) exist, but the largest scale may still be uniform.
                                                • Isotropy: the universe looks about the same in every direction. It is independent of homogeneity.
                                                • These are simple assumptions to build a model; comparing the model with reality shows how the real one differs.

                                                The cosmic microwave background and Olbers’ paradox

                                                From the notes
                                                • In 1965 microwave radiation was detected from all directions. Its spectrum is a black body of 3 K. After correction it is homogeneous and isotropic to a very high precision. It was produced when the universe was very young, so the principles held at the beginning.
                                                • Olbers’ paradox: “Why is the night sky dark?” If the universe were infinite and uniformly filled with stars, every line of sight would end on a star and the sky would be as bright as the day (like looking into a forest). Resolution: looking far away is looking back in time; we would see back to a time when stars had not formed. This needs the universe to have a finite age.
                                                Think like the exam

                                                Check the CMB with Wien’s law: λpeak = 2.9 × 10⁶ nm·K ÷ 3 K ≈ 10⁶ nm = 1 mm: the microwave band. A 3 K black body peaks in microwaves, which is why it is called a microwave background. Many “simple but wrong” answers to Olbers’ paradox (dust absorbs the light, the sky is too far) fail because only the finite age works.

                                                Redshift and Hubble’s law

                                                From the notes
                                                • Most galaxies show cosmological redshift: z = (observed − original wavelength) ÷ original wavelength. Example: 515 nm observed vs 500 nm in the lab gives z = 0.03.
                                                • For small z it is a Doppler effect; v = zc (z much less than 1). That example: about 9000 km/s receding.
                                                • If motions were random, half the galaxies would approach. Hubble found most are receding and the speed is proportional to distance: v = H d (Hubble’s law). H is hard to measure: 50 to 100 km/s/Mpc, best value about 67 km/s/Mpc. The example galaxy is about 134 Mpc away.
                                                Think like the exam

                                                Chain of calculation: wavelengths → z → v = zc → d = v/H. Convert Mpc to light-years (1 Mpc = 3.26 million ly). The expansion has no centre: every galaxy sees all others receding (the raisins in rising bread). Also 1/H is a rough age: 977.8 ÷ 67 = 14.6 billion years.

                                                The Big Bang

                                                From the notes
                                                • The simplest model agreeing with the principles and observations. At about 1 millisecond after the start, the universe was very small, extremely dense and hot (about 10¹² K). It expanded and cooled; the 3 K background is what is left.
                                                • Like baking bread: the loaf grows and the raisins move apart; from any raisin, all others recede, the farther the faster.
                                                • Redshift: light from a far galaxy spends a long time travelling while the universe expands and stretches the photons’ wavelengths.
                                                • Age: run the movie backward to when the galaxies were together: about 14 billion years (1.4 × 10¹⁰ yr).

                                                Evolution and the future

                                                From the notes
                                                • We do not know how large-scale structure and galaxies formed. In the first million years photons far outnumbered matter; after about a million years matter dominated and structure began to form.
                                                • The future depends on the sum of the mass density and the dark energy density (energy of the vacuum, the cosmological constant) compared with the critical density (calculated from H). Larger: it stops expanding and collapses: closed (finite, like a sphere’s surface). Smaller: expands for ever: open (infinite, hyperbolic geometry). Equal: flat.
                                                • We do not know what dark energy is, nor the nature of dark matter. Distant Type Ia supernovae suggest the total density is about critical (flat) and that the expansion is accelerating.
                                                Think like the exam

                                                Keep the three words straight: closed → recollapse, open → expand for ever, flat → just balanced; and keep dark matter (extra gravity from unseen stuff) separate from dark energy (vacuum energy; pushes expansion to accelerate).

                                                Practise

                                                A galaxy’s hydrogen line (rest 656.3 nm) is seen at 662.9 nm. Find v and d (H = 67).
                                                z = 6.6 / 656.3 = 0.01006; v = zc ≈ 3000 km/s; d = 3000 / 67 ≈ 45 Mpc.
                                                Does the Hubble expansion mean we are at the centre?
                                                No. Uniform expansion looks the same from every galaxy (raisin bread).
                                                Why is the night sky dark?
                                                The universe has a finite age, so far away we look back to a time before stars formed: not every line of sight ends on a star.
                                                What does the microwave background tell us?
                                                The universe began hot and dense; it has cooled to about 3 K; it is isotropic and homogeneous to high precision.
                                                Closed, open and flat: which will expand for ever?
                                                Open (and flat, slowing). Closed will recollapse.

                                                Chapter 19 must-know list

                                                • Universality, homogeneity, isotropy. Local Group, Local Supercluster, Great Wall, voids.
                                                • CMB: 1965, 3 K black body. Olbers’ paradox: finite age.
                                                • z = Δλ/λ; v = zc; v = Hd; H ≈ 67 km/s/Mpc; age ≈ 14 billion years.
                                                • Big Bang: 1 ms, 10¹² K; expansion stretches wavelengths. Closed / open / flat; dark energy; accelerating expansion.

                                                  Writing answers that earn every mark

                                                  Four templates
                                                  • Explain why: “Because [cause]. This means [mechanism]. So [result].” Example: Venus has a thick CO₂ atmosphere (90 bar), which traps heat (greenhouse effect), so the whole surface is about 500 °C, hotter than Mercury even though Mercury is closer.
                                                  • Compare: use the same property for both sides. “Earth’s day is 24 h; Mars’s is about 25 h (similar). Earth’s atmosphere is 78% N₂; Mars’s is 95% CO₂ (different).”
                                                  • Calculate: Formula → numbers → answer + unit → meaning. Even if the arithmetic slips, the method marks survive.
                                                  • Comment on a statement: “This is false/partly true. The correct statement is… because…”.
                                                  Common mark-losers
                                                  • Forgetting units (pc vs ly, AU, arcseconds, degrees).
                                                  • Saying “brighter” when the question is about luminosity (true power) rather than apparent brightness.
                                                  • Using the magnitude scale backwards (smaller number = brighter).
                                                  • Stating a result without its reason (“it is hot because it is close”).
                                                  • Giving two points when three are asked, or merging two ideas into one point.

                                                  Short-answer bank with model answers

                                                  Cover the answer, write your own in two or three sentences, then open it.

                                                  Why is Venus hotter than Mercury, even though Mercury is nearer the Sun?
                                                  Venus has a very thick CO₂ atmosphere (about 90 bar). It lets sunlight in but traps the infrared heat the surface radiates (greenhouse effect), and winds spread the heat, so the whole surface is about 500 °C day and night. Mercury has almost no atmosphere, so it cannot trap or spread heat: hot by day, very cold by night.
                                                  Mars is cold but has seasons; Venus is hot but has none. Explain both.
                                                  Seasons come from axis tilt: Mars is tilted about 24°, similar to Earth, so each hemisphere alternately leans toward or away from the Sun. Venus is tilted almost 0° (spin axis nearly perpendicular to its orbit), so every latitude gets the same sunlight all year. Temperature on each is set by the atmosphere (thin on Mars, thick on Venus), not by seasons.
                                                  Saturn’s density is below water. What does it tell us about its composition? Would Saturn float?
                                                  Mostly light gases, hydrogen and helium, with only a small rocky core. A body of density 0.7 relative to water would float if a big enough bowl of water existed. Terrestrial planets (density 4 to 5.5) are rocky and metallic.
                                                  A comet’s nucleus is under 10 km but its tail may be millions of km long. How?
                                                  When the comet nears the Sun, heating makes the ice sublimate. The released gas and dust form a huge coma and then a tail, pushed away from the Sun by the solar wind and radiation pressure. The tail is extremely thin: huge in size, tiny in mass.
                                                  A comet is seen with its tail pointing to the right. Which way is it moving?
                                                  You cannot tell from the tail. The tail points away from the Sun, wherever the comet is going. The Sun is on the left. Heading toward the Sun, the tail trails behind it; heading away, the tail leads.
                                                  Is Pluto a planet? Use criteria.
                                                  Criteria: (1) orbits the Sun: yes; (2) massive enough to be round: yes; (3) has cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit: no (its orbit shares space with Kuiper belt objects, and it crosses Neptune’s). So it is a dwarf planet. It is also smaller than our Moon (2370 km).
                                                  Meteoroid, meteor, meteorite: what is the difference?
                                                  Meteoroid: a small rock in space. Meteor: the streak of light when it burns up in the atmosphere. Meteorite: the part that survives and lands on the ground.
                                                  Why doesn’t the Sun collapse under its own gravity or explode from fusion?
                                                  Hydrostatic balance. Gravity pulls in; the pressure of the hot gas and radiation pushes out. If fusion speeds up, the core expands and cools, slowing fusion; if it slows, the core contracts and heats, speeding fusion. Result: a stable thermostat.
                                                  Sunspots are about 4000 K, yet they look black. Why?
                                                  They are cooler than the 6000 K photosphere. Energy emitted per unit area goes as T⁴, so a 4000 K patch emits only (4000/6000)⁴ ≈ 20% as much. Next to the bright surroundings, it looks dark. Alone, it would shine brilliantly.
                                                  Why can’t we use apparent magnitude to say which star gives out more energy?
                                                  Apparent magnitude depends on both the star’s true power (luminosity) and its distance. A faint star close by can look brighter than a powerful star far away. To compare true power we use absolute magnitude: how bright the star would look at a standard distance of 10 pc.
                                                  Why does parallax work only for nearby stars?
                                                  Parallax shrinks as 1/distance. Distant stars shift by angles too small to measure from the ground (Gaia in space measures far smaller angles, so it reaches farther). For more distant stars we use other methods, such as Cepheids.
                                                  How do Cepheids give distances?
                                                  Period tells luminosity: longer period = more luminous. Measure the period → know M. Measure apparent magnitude m → compute d from m − M = 5 log(d/10 pc). They are bright enough to be seen in other galaxies, so they work as “standard candles”.
                                                  Why do stars in a cluster make good test subjects for theories of star lives?
                                                  All the stars are at the same distance (so differences in apparent magnitude equal differences in luminosity) and formed at the same time from the same cloud (so differences come from mass alone).
                                                  A protostar shines brightly. Why isn’t it a star?
                                                  A star must make its own energy by nuclear fusion in its core. A protostar shines from gravitational contraction energy. Fusion begins only when the core reaches about 10 million K, which needs at least about 0.1 solar mass; below that the object is a brown dwarf.
                                                  Why do massive stars live a much shorter time although they have more fuel?
                                                  They burn it far faster. Luminosity rises steeply with mass (roughly as M³ to M⁴), while fuel increases only as M. Lifetime ∝ fuel ÷ burn rate, so the more massive star runs out much sooner: the most massive live around a million years, small ones tens of billions.

                                                  Question 1 (40 marks)

                                                  (a) Venus is hotter than Mercury although it is farther from the Sun. Explain. [10]

                                                  (b) Describe how Saturn’s rings are believed to have formed, and name one feature of the ring system that is explained by a nearby small moon. [15]

                                                  (c) Saturn has a mass of 95 Earth masses and a diameter of 9.4 Earth diameters. Find its average density in water = 1 units (Earth’s density is 5.5 times water), and comment on what it tells you. [15]

                                                  Model answer and marking points: Q1

                                                  (a) [10] Thick CO₂ atmosphere (90 bar) [2]; traps infrared heat from the surface, greenhouse effect [4]; winds spread heat so day and night are the same, about 500 °C [2]; Mercury has almost no atmosphere, so it loses heat, with a large day–night swing [2].

                                                  (b) [15] A satellite came too close to Saturn and was torn apart by tidal forces [5]; the debris (dust and ice, mm to metres) spread into a thin flat ring [5]; shepherd satellites on both sides of a narrow ring keep it confined [5] (or the Cassini division from the resonance with a moon).

                                                  (c) [15] ρ = 5.5 × 95 / 9.4³ [5] = 5.5 × 95 / 830.6 = 0.63 [5] (water = 1). Lower than water, so Saturn is mainly hydrogen and helium gas, not rock; it would float [5].

                                                  Question 2 (30 marks)

                                                  (a) A comet is observed with its tail pointing to the right and the Sun is on its left. Can you tell whether it is moving toward or away from the Sun? Explain what the tail is. [10]

                                                  (b) Halley’s Comet has an orbital period of 76 years and a closest approach (perihelion) of 0.59 AU. Find its average distance and its farthest distance from the Sun. [10]

                                                  (c) A sunspot has a temperature of 4000 K, against 6000 K for the surrounding photosphere. Why does it look dark? Estimate how its brightness per unit area compares. [10]

                                                  Model answer and marking points: Q2

                                                  (a) [10] No, you cannot [3]. The tail always points away from the Sun [3] because the solar wind and radiation pressure push the gas and dust that the heated nucleus releases [4].

                                                  (b) [10] P² = a³ → a = 762/3 = 17.9 AU [5]. Perihelion + aphelion = 2a → aphelion = 35.9 − 0.59 = 35.3 AU [5].

                                                  (c) [10] The sunspot is cooler than the surroundings [3]; energy per area ∝ T⁴ [3], so ratio = (4000/6000)⁴ = 0.2 [4]: about 20% of the neighbouring brightness, so it looks dark by contrast.

                                                  Question 3 (30 marks)

                                                  A star X has apparent magnitude 8.0 and a measured parallax of 0.05 arcsec.

                                                  (a) Find its distance in parsecs and in light-years. [6]

                                                  (b) Find its absolute magnitude. [12]

                                                  (c) The Sun’s absolute magnitude is 4.8. Which is more luminous, and by what factor? [12]

                                                  Model answer and marking points: Q3

                                                  (a) d = 1/0.05 = 20 pc [3] = 20 × 3.26 = 65.2 ly [3].

                                                  (b) m − M = 5 log(d/10) [4] → M = 8.0 − 5 log(2) [4] = 8.0 − 1.51 = 6.49 [4].

                                                  (c) The Sun, since its M (4.8) is smaller [4]. ΔM = 6.49 − 4.8 = 1.69 [4]; ratio = 100.4 × 1.69 ≈ 4.8, so the Sun is about 4.8 times more luminous than X [4].

                                                  (b) [15] A star needs fusion in its core [3]; this needs a core temperature of about 10 million K, which needs a mass of at least about 0.1 solar mass [4]. Jupiter is only about 0.001 solar mass, so its core never reaches that [4]. It still gives out internal heat left from formation and slow contraction [4], so it radiates more than it receives from the Sun.

                                                  (a) What does a smaller magnitude number mean for a star? How did this scale originate? [8]

                                                  (b) Star P has apparent magnitude 1.0 and star Q has 3.5. Which looks brighter, and by what factor? [10]

                                                  (c) If star P were moved to twice its distance, by how many magnitudes would it change? [12]

                                                  (b) [10] P looks brighter [3]. Δm = 2.5, so ratio = 100.4 × 2.5 = 10 [7].

                                                  Question 3 (30 marks)

                                                  (a) State two properties that all stars in a cluster share, and say why this is useful. [10]

                                                  (b) State three differences between open and globular clusters. [10]

                                                  (c) A star in a cluster has apparent magnitude 5.0 and the cluster is 25 pc away. Find the star’s absolute magnitude, and compare its luminosity with the Sun’s (M = 4.8). [10]

                                                  Model answer and marking points: B3

                                                  (a) [10] Same distance and same age (formed from the same cloud) [4]. So differences in apparent magnitude are real differences in luminosity, and differences between stars come from mass alone, which allows testing of star-life theories [6].

                                                  (b) [10] Open: hundreds of stars, young, irregular/loose (e.g. the Pleiades M45). Globular: more than 10,000 stars, old, compact and concentrated (e.g. M80). Four marks for each of two matching pairs and two for the third.

                                                  Question 1 (40 marks)

                                                  Planet Q orbits a Sun-like star. Its axis tilt is 2°, it has a CO₂ atmosphere 50 times as thick as Earth’s, and it spins backwards once every 200 Earth days while its year is 150 Earth days.

                                                  (a) Predict, with reasons: does Q have seasons? Is its day–night temperature difference large or small? Would you expect many craters? [15]

                                                  (b) Find the length of Q’s solar day (sunrise to sunrise). In which direction does its sun rise? [10]

                                                  (c) Q has one moon, 2000 km across, 300,000 km away. Its sun looks 0.5° across from Q. Can Q see total solar eclipses? If Q had a second, identical moon always on the opposite side, how many tidal bulges would its oceans have? [15]

                                                  Model answer and marking points: C1

                                                  (a) [15] Tilt only 2°, so the sun stays within 2° of the equator all year: no real seasons [5] (like Venus). Very thick CO₂ atmosphere: strong greenhouse effect traps heat and the thick air spreads it, so the day–night difference is small and the surface is hot [5]. The thick atmosphere slows and burns up meteoroids, so few craters [5] (Venus vs Mercury).

                                                  (b) [10] Spin opposite to orbit: 1/solar = 1/sidereal + 1/year [4] = 1/200 + 1/150 = 0.0050 + 0.00667 = 0.01167 → solar day ≈ 86 Earth days [3], shorter than the spin. The sun rises in the west because the spin is backwards [3].

                                                  (c) [15] Moon’s angular size = 2000 ÷ 300,000 = 0.00667 rad × 57.3 = 0.38° [5]. That is smaller than the sun’s 0.5°, so the moon cannot cover the whole sun: only annular (and partial) eclipses, no total eclipse [4]. Two moons on opposite sides: each makes a bulge toward it and one on the far side, and these coincide, so there are still two bulges (larger ones) [6] (Assignment 2 Q3).

                                                  Star P’s spectrum peaks in the blue; star Q’s peaks in the red. Both have apparent magnitude 6.0. P’s parallax is 0.01″; Q’s is 0.002″.

                                                  (a) Which star has the higher surface temperature? Explain. [4]

                                                  (b) Find the absolute magnitude of each star. Which is more luminous, and by how many times? [14]

                                                  (c) Explain why Q must be much larger than P, and say where each lies on the H-R diagram. [8]

                                                  (d) A line in Q’s spectrum that is at 500.0 nm in the laboratory is observed at 499.5 nm. What does this tell you about Q’s motion? Does it tell you Q’s proper motion? [4]

                                                  Model answer and marking points: C2

                                                    Q1. Brightness ratio to magnitude

                                                    Question

                                                    The brightness of star A is 317 times that of star B and the visual magnitude of star B is 7.2. What is the visual magnitude of star A?

                                                    Model answer

                                                    Magnitude difference: mB − mA = 2.5 log₁₀(bA/bB) = 2.5 × log₁₀(317) = 2.5 × 2.501 = 6.25.

                                                    A is brighter, so its magnitude is smaller: mA = 7.2 − 6.25 = 0.95 (about 1.0).

                                                    Think like the exam

                                                    Quick check before the calculator: 317 is a bit more than 100, and 100× = 5 magnitudes, so the answer should be a bit more than 5 below 7.2, i.e. near 1. The usual wrong answer is 7.2 + 6.25 = 13.45, which would make the brighter star fainter. Always ask: “is my star brighter or fainter than the reference?”

                                                    Q2. Black body and surface temperature

                                                    Question

                                                    What is a black body? If a star radiates like a black body, how can one estimate its surface temperature?

                                                    Model answer

                                                    Black body: an ideal object that absorbs all radiation falling on it and, because it is hot and dense, radiates a continuous spectrum that depends only on its temperature. Stars and glowing metal are close to this.

                                                    Estimating T: (1) measure the star’s spectrum (or colour) and find the wavelength λmax where the intensity peaks. (2) The hotter the star, the shorter the peak wavelength (red → yellow → blue → ultraviolet). (3) Wien’s law: T = 2.9 × 10⁶ nm·K ÷ λmax. Example: a peak at 500 nm gives T ≈ 5800 K, like the Sun.

                                                    Extra marks: hotter objects also radiate more energy per unit area (energy ∝ T⁴), so if the luminosity and radius are known, T can also be found from L = 4πR²σT⁴.

                                                    Official solution: the key points

                                                    Absorbs all radiation that hits it and emits a spectrum with a peak; the spectrum depends only on the surface temperature, not on the chemical composition or shape; warm → peak in the red, hotter → yellow; no real object is a perfect black body but a star comes very close; so estimate the temperature from the star’s colour, or better from its spectrum. Wien’s law and the T⁴ rule above are extra (not in the notes): fine to add, never required.

                                                    Think like the exam

                                                    The notes say the spectrum depends only on the surface temperature, and that colour mainly reflects temperature while luminosity depends on both temperature and surface area. The question asks “how”, so describe a method (measure, find the peak, convert), not just “look at the colour”.

                                                    Q3. Two moons and the number of high tides

                                                    Question

                                                    If the Earth has two moons and they are always on the opposite sides of the Earth, how many high tides do we have in one day? Why?

                                                    Model answer

                                                    Still two high tides a day. One moon pulls hardest on the near-side water, less on the Earth, least on the far-side water, so there are two bulges: one facing the moon and one on the opposite side. With two moons on opposite sides, the near-side bulge of Moon 1 is exactly where the far-side bulge of Moon 2 is, and vice versa. The bulges coincide rather than forming four, so the high tides are about twice as high, still two per day.

                                                    Trap

                                                    “Two moons × two bulges = four tides” is the tempting answer. The wording “always on the opposite sides” is the clue that the bulges overlap. Try the slider: for any angle between the moons the pattern is still a single pair of bulges; at 90° two equal moons even cancel out.

                                                    Q4. Comment on eclipse statements

                                                    Question

                                                    (a) “Lunar eclipses never occur during a crescent moon.” (b) “Although solar eclipses are extremely rare on the day prior to Mid-Autumn Festival, it has been recorded at least twice in history.”

                                                    Model answer (a): true

                                                    A lunar eclipse happens when the Moon enters the Earth’s shadow, so the Moon must be on the far side of the Earth from the Sun: full moon. A crescent moon is near the Sun in the sky, on the lit-side-toward-Sun end of its orbit, so the Earth’s shadow cannot reach it. (A partly eclipsed Moon can look like a bite, but its phase is still full.)

                                                    Model answer (b): false

                                                    A solar eclipse needs the Moon between the Earth and the Sun: new moon. Mid-Autumn Festival is the 15th day of the 8th lunar month, at about full moon, so the day before is about 13 days after new moon. On that day the Moon is almost full: nearly behind the Earth, on the side opposite the Sun. It takes almost half a month for the Moon to move from the front of the Earth (needed for a solar eclipse) to the back (full moon), so a solar eclipse on the day before Mid-Autumn Festival could never occur. The statement is false. (Official solution, word for word in substance.)

                                                    Think like the exam

                                                    “Comment on” means: decide true or false, then give the reason from the notes. Here the notes give the reasons: lunar eclipse = Moon behind the Earth; solar eclipse = Moon between Sun and Earth (new moon), and phases follow the Sun–Earth–Moon geometry.

                                                    Q6. Titan, the Sun, and a total eclipse on Saturn

                                                    Question

                                                    (Look up the necessary data.) (a) The average angular size of Titan as viewed on Saturn? (b) The angular size of the Sun as viewed on Saturn? (c) Can we see a total solar eclipse by Titan, if they are on the same orbital plane?

                                                    Data used: Titan diameter 5150 km; Titan–Saturn distance 1.22 × 10⁶ km; Sun diameter 1.39 × 10⁶ km; Saturn–Sun distance 9.58 AU = 1.43 × 10⁹ km.
                                                    (a) Small-angle formula: θ = D/d = 5150 / 1.22 × 10⁶ = 4.2 × 10⁻³ rad = 4.2 × 10⁻³ × 57.3 ≈ 0.24° (about 14 arcminutes).
                                                    (b) θ = 1.39 × 10⁶ / 1.43 × 10⁹ = 9.7 × 10⁻⁴ rad ≈ 0.056° (about 3.3 arcminutes). The Sun is a tiny bright dot on Saturn’s sky compared with Titan.
                                                    (c) Yes. Titan’s angular size (0.24°) is about 4 times larger than the Sun’s (0.056°), so when they line up Titan can cover the Sun completely and stay in front of it for a long time. (On Earth the Moon and Sun are almost equal, about 0.5° each, which is why total eclipses are short and sometimes only annular.)
                                                    Trap

                                                    Use the same units on top and bottom (km / km). Convert the AU distance into km, and convert the angle from radians to degrees (× 57.3).                                                     Q7. Dwarf planet criteria

                                                    Question

                                                    On 24 August 2006 the IAU downgraded Pluto from an official planet to a dwarf planet. Look up the criteria of being a dwarf planet.

                                                    Model answer

                                                    A dwarf planet is an object that (1) orbits the Sun; (2) has enough mass for its own gravity to make it nearly round; (3) has not cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit of other objects; and (4) is not a satellite of another body. A full planet meets 1, 2 and the opposite of 3 (it has cleared its orbit). Pluto fails criterion 3: its orbit overlaps the Kuiper belt and crosses Neptune’s, so it is a dwarf planet.

                                                    Q8. What is a star, and why is the Sun one?

                                                    Question

                                                    What is a star and why is our Sun a star?

                                                    Model answer

                                                    A star is a huge, hot ball of gas held together by its own gravity, which makes its own energy by nuclear fusion in its core and shines by it. The Sun fits each part: it is a ball of gas (70% hydrogen), held together by its own gravity, with a core hotter than 10⁷ K where hydrogen fuses into helium (p-p chain), and it shines by its own light. In contrast, Jupiter is also a gas ball but is too light to ignite fusion, and a planet only reflects light.

                                                    Q10. Parallax to absolute magnitude, then compare with the Sun

                                                      Q1. Distance from angular size (10 marks)

                                                      Question

                                                      Object A is 15.0 AU away with angular size 34.5″. Object B has an angular size 2.42 times A’s and a diameter 1.32 × 10⁸ times A’s. How far away is B?

                                                      Method

                                                      For small angles, diameter = angular size (in radians) × distance, so distance = diameter ÷ angular size. Take the ratio B to A: dB/dA = (LB/LA) ÷ (aB/aA) = 1.32 × 10⁸ ÷ 2.42 = 5.45 × 10⁷.

                                                      dB = 5.45 × 10⁷ × 15.0 AU = 8.18 × 10⁸ AU ≈ 13,000 light-years (1 ly = 63,241 AU).

                                                      Think like the exam

                                                      The 34.5″ is a distraction: it cancels. Whenever two objects are compared, work with ratios: bigger real size → farther, bigger angle → nearer. Direction check: B is enormously bigger but only 2.4 times larger in angle, so it must be very far.

                                                      Q2. Angular size of the Sun (5 marks)

                                                      Question

                                                      Look up the mean radius of the Sun and its mean distance from Earth, then calculate the Sun’s angular size.

                                                      Answer

                                                      Radius 6.96 × 10⁸ m; distance 1.50 × 10¹¹ m. Angular diameter = 2 × tan⁻¹(6.96 × 10⁸ / 1.50 × 10¹¹) = 2 × 0.2659° = 0.53°. (Small-angle shortcut: 2R/d = 9.3 × 10⁻³ rad × 57.3 = 0.53°.)

                                                      Trap

                                                      The angle needs the diameter (2R), not the radius, or 2 × the half-angle. Using R/d alone gives half the right answer (0.27°).

                                                      Q3. Where the Sun rises through the year (5 + 5 marks)

                                                      Question

                                                      Explain, with diagrams, the Sun’s rising direction in Hong Kong from (a) the vernal equinox to the summer solstice, (b) the autumnal equinox to the winter solstice.

                                                      Model answer

                                                      (a) At the vernal equinox the Sun is on the celestial equator, so it rises due east. As it moves north on the celestial sphere toward the summer solstice, its rising point moves north of east. At the summer solstice it rises at the northernmost point of the year (about 26° north of east in Hong Kong).

                                                      (b) After the autumnal equinox the Sun rises due east, then its rising point moves south of east as the Sun moves south, reaching the southernmost rising point (about 26° south of east) at the winter solstice.

                                                      Think like the exam

                                                      A diagram is requested: draw a horizon circle from above (N up, E right) and mark due east, the northern-most and southern-most rising points. The link is simple: declination of the Sun decides where it rises. Equator = due east, north declination = north of east.

                                                      Q4. Southernmost circumpolar stars in Hong Kong (10 marks)

                                                      Question

                                                      What is the declination of the southernmost circumpolar stars as observed in Hong Kong?

                                                      Answer

                                                      The north celestial pole is 22.5° above the horizon in Hong Kong (altitude of the pole = latitude). A star never sets if its angular distance from the pole is less than 22.5°; it just touches the horizon at 22.5° from the pole. Declination = 90° − 22.5° = 67.5° N.

                                                      Think like the exam

                                                      Variant: Beijing (40° N): 50° N. Southern hemisphere, e.g. Sydney (34° S): stars with declination south of −56°. Rule: circumpolar if declination ≥ 90° − latitude (same hemisphere as the pole).

                                                      Q5. Leap years (10 marks)

                                                      Question

                                                      (a) Is there a 29 February in 2104? (b) By the Gregorian calendar, how many leap years from 1600 to 2500 inclusive?

                                                      Answer

                                                      (a) Yes. 2104 is divisible by 4 and is not a century year, so it is a leap year.

                                                      (b) 219. Rule: divisible by 4, except century years not divisible by 400. Multiples of 4 from 1600 to 2500: (2500 − 1600)/4 + 1 = 226. Remove century years that are not multiples of 400: 1700, 1800, 1900, 2100, 2200, 2300, 2500 (7 of them; 1600 and 2400 stay). 226 − 7 = 219.

                                                      Trap

                                                      Counting inclusive ends: both 1600 and 2500 are in the range. 2500 is a century year not divisible by 400, so it is not a leap year, even though it is a multiple of 4.

                                                      Q6. Equinox, zenith, meridian, and the Sun overhead (15 marks)

                                                      Question

                                                      (a) Explain: equinoxes, zenith, meridian. (b) How many times a year does the Sun pass through the zenith of Hong Kong, Beijing, Singapore?

                                                      (a) Equinoxes: the two points where the celestial equator and the ecliptic cross (the Sun is there around 21 March and 23 September). Zenith: the point directly above the observer. Meridian: the great circle through the north and south celestial poles and the zenith.
                                                      (b) Hong Kong 2, Beijing 0, Singapore 2.
                                                      Think like the exam

                                                      The reason: the Sun’s declination only ranges between −23.5° and +23.5°, and a place’s zenith has declination equal to its latitude. A place inside the tropics sees the Sun overhead twice a year (once going north, once going south); on the Tropic itself, once; outside, never.

                                                      Q7. Reversed rotation (10 marks)

                                                      Question

                                                      If the Earth’s rotation were reversed and the revolution stayed the same, would the sidereal day be longer than the solar day?

                                                      Answer: yes. In reality the Earth spins the same way as it orbits, so after one full spin (a sidereal day) it still has to turn a little more (about 4 minutes) to face the Sun again, since it has moved along its orbit. The solar day is longer. If the spin were reversed, the orbital motion would carry the Earth toward the Sun’s direction: it would meet the Sun again before a full spin. Solar day shorter, so the sidereal day is longer. In numbers: 1/solar = 1/sidereal + 1/year.

                                                      Q8. Something Ptolemy cannot explain (10 marks)

                                                      Question

                                                      State one phenomenon that the Ptolemaic geocentric model cannot explain but the heliocentric model can.

                                                      Answer

                                                      The full set of phases of Venus. In Ptolemy’s model, Venus’s epicycle centre stays on the line between the Earth and the Sun, so Venus is always between us and the Sun and could only show crescent or new phases. Galileo saw Venus go through all phases, including nearly full. In the heliocentric model Venus orbits the Sun, so it can be on the far side of the Sun (full) or on our side (crescent).

                                                      Trap

                                                      Retrograde motion is not a valid answer: Ptolemy’s epicycles explain it (that is exactly what Test 1 Q2 asks).

                                                      Q9. The point where Earth’s and Moon’s pulls are equal (10 marks)

                                                      Question

                                                      Estimate the distance from the Earth’s centre of a point between Earth and Moon where the pull of the Moon equals the pull of the Earth. Earth–Moon distance 3.84 × 10⁸ m; mass ratio 81.

                                                      Answer

                                                      G Me/r² = G Mm/(R − r)². With Me/Mm = 81, take square roots: r/(R − r) = 9, so r = 9R/10 = 3.46 × 10⁸ m from the Earth’s centre (about 90% of the way to the Moon, since Earth is the heavy one and its pull has to be diluted by distance). The quadratic’s other root, 9R/8, lies beyond the Moon and is rejected.

                                                      Think like the exam

                                                      Taking the square root straight away is faster than solving a quadratic: distance ratio = √(mass ratio). Sanity check: the balance point must lie much closer to the lighter body, because the heavier body’s stronger pull has to be weakened by a longer distance. Here r = 0.9 R leaves only 0.1 R to the Moon. A result in the middle (0.5 R) would be wrong.

                                                      Q10. Kepler’s third law (10 marks)

                                                      Question

                                                      A star has planets A and B. A’s average orbital radius is 3.63 AU, the ratio of the periods is 54.1, and A’s period is shorter. Find B’s radius.

                                                      Answer

                                                      Kepler’s third law: P² ∝ a³, so (aB/aA)³ = (PB/PA)² = 54.1². Then aB/aA = 54.12/3 = 14.3, so aB = 3.63 × 14.3 ≈ 51.9 AU (B is farther because it has the longer period).

                                                      Think like the exam

                                                      The ratio form means that no units for the star’s mass are needed. This is the same tool used for comets in Chapter 10 (Halley’s 76-year orbit gives 17.9 AU).

                                                        Question 1 (40 marks): how stars move at different latitudes

                                                        Question

                                                        (a) At the south pole, describe clearly how stars move across the sky in one day. (b) At a location on the equator, for stars exactly due east, do they rise vertically? (c) In Hong Kong (22.5° N), for stars at exactly due east, do they rise vertically?

                                                        The whole question follows from one idea: the sky turns around an axis that points to the celestial pole, and that axis is tilted from the horizon by an angle equal to the latitude.

                                                        (a) At the south pole

                                                        Full-marks answer

                                                        At the south pole the south celestial pole is at the zenith. Every star moves in a circle parallel to the horizon, keeping the same altitude, so none rises or sets. Stars near the horizon move sideways from right to left, one full turn per day. Looking up, stars around the zenith circle clockwise about the zenith. (Only stars of the southern celestial hemisphere are ever seen.)

                                                        Trap

                                                        The solution notes that “just answering clockwise or anti-clockwise is not clear enough”. Say from where you look (up at the zenith or at the horizon) and what stays constant (altitude). Marks go to a clear description, not one word.

                                                        (b) and (c) Rising angle at the equator and in Hong Kong

                                                        Answers

                                                        (b) Yes. At the equator the Earth’s rotation axis is horizontal (both celestial poles are on the horizon), so all stars rise vertically.

                                                        (c) No. In Hong Kong the north celestial pole is 22.5° above the horizon, so the rotation axis is tilted 22.5° from the horizontal. A star at due east rises at 22.5° from vertical, tilted toward the south (to the right when facing east).

                                                        Think like the exam

                                                        The applied rule: the angle from vertical at which a due-east star rises equals the latitude. At the poles that is 90°: the path is horizontal, matching part (a). Variants to be ready for: Beijing (40°: 40° from vertical); “what is the altitude of the north celestial pole at Singapore?” (1.3°); “at what latitude would a due-east star rise at 45° from vertical?” (45°).

                                                        Question 2 (30 marks): retrograde motion

                                                        Question

                                                        (a) Briefly describe retrograde motion of planets. (b) How can it be explained in the Ptolemaic model? (c) In the heliocentric model?

                                                        (a) Describe

                                                        Against the background stars, planets normally move from west to east. Once in a while a planet slows, reverses and moves from east to west for some weeks (retrograde motion), then reverses again and resumes the normal west-to-east motion.

                                                        (b) Ptolemaic model

                                                        Each planet moves on a small circle (the epicycle) whose centre moves on a big circle (the deferent) around the Earth. When the planet is on the part of the epicycle nearest the Earth, its motion on the epicycle is opposite to the deferent’s motion, so it appears to move backward for a while.

                                                        (c) Heliocentric model

                                                        All planets orbit the Sun, the inner ones faster. When the Earth overtakes an outer planet such as Mars, our line of sight to the planet swings backward against the distant stars, like a faster car overtaking a slower car and making it seem to move backward against the scenery. Retrograde motion is just the relative motion of the Earth and the planet; it happens near opposition, when the planet is opposite the Sun.

                                                        Think like the exam

                                                        Parts (b) and (c) test whether you can state the mechanism, one sentence each: “epicycle on a deferent” and “Earth overtakes the planet”. Also know what they cannot do: Ptolemy’s model can explain retrograde motion (so it is not a good answer to Assignment 1 Q8), but it needs the epicycles added by hand; in the heliocentric model it arises naturally.

                                                        Question 3 (30 marks): telescopes

                                                        Question

                                                        (a) Name the primary optical element of a refracting telescope. (b) Define the magnification of a telescope. (c) Why do astronomers not increase the magnification to a large value, e.g. 1000?

                                                        Answers

                                                        (a) A lens (the objective lens). A reflector uses a mirror, and a catadioptric telescope uses both a lens (correcting plate) and a mirror.

                                                        (b) Magnification = angular size of the image ÷ angular size of the object. For example, an object 1 arcminute across that looks 30 arcminutes across has magnification 30.

                                                        (c) At very high magnification the image is large but very fuzzy: the detail is limited by the telescope’s resolution (and by the atmosphere), so enlarging further adds no detail, while the image gets dimmer and less sharp. Its quality decreases. In practice magnification is seldom above 500 and usually 100–200.

                                                        Think like the exam

                                                        The notes correct a common misconception: a larger telescope does not magnify more. Magnification is changed just by swapping the eyepiece. What a larger aperture gives is a brighter image (more light collected, area grows as diameter²) and a sharper one (better resolution). Expect variants like “Why do astronomers build large telescopes?” (collect more light, improve resolution) or “How far can this telescope see?” (not well defined: the right question is how dim an object it can detect).

                                                        How to read what the marker wants

                                                        • “Describe clearly” → give at least two features (direction and what stays constant).
                                                        • “Do they…?” → answer Yes or No first, then give the reason in one sentence.
                                                        • “Define” → a ratio or sentence a stranger could use; no vague word like “bigger”.
                                                        • “Why not…?” → state the physical limit (fuzzy image, resolution), not just “it is not useful”.
